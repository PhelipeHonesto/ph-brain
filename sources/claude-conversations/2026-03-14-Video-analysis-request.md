**The core thesis — "BAD BUSINESS":** The thumbnail and title tell you everything. Ryan is arguing that starting a private jet charter brokerage is *not* the gold mine social media makes it look like. From what I've pieced together, his main points likely include:

**1. Low barriers to entry = race to the bottom.** Charter brokerage requires no Part 135 certificate, no aircraft, and minimal startup capital. You're essentially a middleman. That means the market is flooded with brokers competing on the same inventory of aircraft, which compresses margins. This aligns with the broader industry reality — while Part 135 charter operators are closely regulated by the FAA, air charter brokers are not comprehensively regulated, making it easy for anyone to hang a shingle.

**2. Commission structure is brutal.** Most jet brokers earn a commission on each charter they book, typically ranging from 1% to 10% of the charter cost. On a $50K charter, that's $500–$5,000 per trip. When you factor in the sales cycle, client acquisition costs, and the fact that deals fall through constantly, the economics get thin fast — especially for a solo operator without an existing book of business.

**3. Client acquisition is the real game — and it's expensive.** UHNW clients don't come from Instagram ads. They come from relationships, referrals, and trust built over years. A new broker with no network is essentially cold-calling into a market where established firms like Air Charter Service and Stratos Jets already have deep operator relationships and brand credibility.

**4. You carry all the risk, own none of the assets.** Unlike an operator who owns or manages aircraft, a broker has no recurring revenue from management fees, no asset appreciation, and no control over the product. If an operator cancels, the broker scrambles. If a client has a bad experience with the operator, the broker takes the blame.

**5. The lifestyle illusion.** Ryan's in a unique position to call this out — he actually *lived* the G650 lifestyle as a pilot. He knows the difference between posting private jet content on social media and actually building a sustainable aviation business.

## What He Got Wrong (or Missed)

This is where it gets interesting for OSA, because Ryan made several structural choices that baked failure into his model from the start:

**1. He ran it as a lead-gen business, not a relationship business.**
Google Ads for charter flights attracts price shoppers by definition. Someone Googling "private jet Miami to New York" is comparison shopping. They'll get quotes from 5 brokers and pick the cheapest. That's not a customer — that's an auction participant. His $91/lead was actually cheap for that keyword space, but the conversion problem isn't cost-per-lead, it's lead *quality*. Cold inbound from search will always commoditize you.

**2. He had no differentiation beyond price.**
He admits this — "a banana is a banana." But that's only true if you *present* yourself as a banana. He had no safety brief, no operator vetting narrative, no transparency model, no content authority, no trust layer. He was selling price against other people selling price.

**3. He had no recurring relationship infrastructure.**
12 leads to close 1 deal, and then what? He doesn't mention repeat business, client retention, or LTV. In charter, the money is in the second, third, tenth flight — not the first. His model was pure acquisition with no retention engine.

**4. He picked the wrong entry point on the value chain.**
Pure brokerage with no adjacent services means you're a one-function middleman. No management, no acquisitions advisory, no operational consulting, no content moat. Just quotes.
