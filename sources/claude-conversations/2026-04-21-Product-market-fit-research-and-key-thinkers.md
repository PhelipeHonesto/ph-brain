## Product-Market Fit: Theory + OSA Diagnosis

**The four people who matter**

Don Valentine (Sequoia) developed the original thinking. Andy Rachleff (Benchmark) named it. Marc Andreessen popularized it. Rahul Vohra (Superhuman) made it measurable. Sean Ellis gave it a number.

**What they actually say**

*Andreessen (2007):* "Product/market fit means being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market." You feel it when customers are buying as fast as you can make it, usage is growing as fast as you can add servers, money is piling up, and reporters are calling. The market pulls the product out of you. Pre-PMF, ignore almost everything else and do whatever is required — change people, rewrite the product, switch markets.

*Rachleff's corollary:* When a great team meets a lousy market, market wins. When a lousy team meets a great market, market wins. Market > team > product. "First to market seldom matters. First to product/market fit is almost always the long-term winner."

*Ellis + Vohra (the leading indicator):* Ask active users "how would you feel if you could no longer use this product?" 40% saying "very disappointed" is the benchmark — below that, companies struggle to grow; above it, they almost always take off. Then segment to find your "very disappointed" users, build a High-Expectation Customer profile, double down on what they love, fix what blocks the "somewhat disappointed" group, and repeat quarterly.

*Lenny Rachitsky (modern B2B):* PMF is a journey with stages — get one company to love it, get them to pay meaningfully, get 3–10 more, then watch push turn to pull. Pupils dilate. Love letters arrive. Customers send you invoices before you send them. Median time from idea to PMF is ~2 years; worry hard past 3.

**How this plays for OSA**

You're in an unusual position — Andreessen's framing says *market* is the whole ballgame. Your market is obviously real: $30B, zero software, EAs and CFOs drowning in PDFs. So the Rachleff/Andreessen test says you're in the right place. Now the question is the Vohra test: *can you get to 40%?*

Three things from this research that change what you should do:

1. **You have two possible PMFs, not one.** Your past conversations already mapped this — B2C first-time charterers score as a "vitamin" (moderate PMF at best), EAs/CFOs score as a "painkiller" (high PMF potential). Vohra's framework says: **stop optimizing for both.** Pick the segment where 40% would be "very disappointed" and build the engine for that one. That's the EA/CFO segment. B2C route pages become acquisition, not the PMF target.

2. **You can't measure PMF yet — and that's the most important fact in this document.** The Ellis survey requires people who've used the product at least twice in two weeks. You have zero charter deals closed. Everything strategic right now (Charter Index, Ghost Positioning, Dumont, Loud Hawk) is pre-PMF theater until you have a handful of customers who've actually transacted and come back. Lenny's milestone 1 is one company that loves it. Milestone 2 is that company paying meaningfully. You haven't hit milestone 1.

3. **The "best people" framework for you specifically:** Andreessen tells you the market is right. Rachleff tells you first-to-PMF wins (not first-to-market — JETNET/Hamilton AI being earlier doesn't matter if you're first to a delighted buyer segment). Vohra gives you the exact instrument to use *the moment you have 40 post-trip customers*. Lenny tells you to obsess over one company until they can't live without you — probably one PE firm's EA, served manually, before you scale anything.

**The one thing to do differently this week**

Design the four-question Ellis/Vohra survey now, *before* you have customers to send it to. Bake it into post-trip flow from deal #1. Your first 40 transactions become your first PMF measurement — not a vanity metric, the only metric that matters pre-Series A.

## Claude

Good — this is the right question to pause on. Picking the wrong segment is how you spend 18 months optimizing for a number that doesn't matter.

Here's the honest breakdown:

**The case for EA/CFO at PE/VC firms**

This is the "painkiller" segment from your past analysis. If you take the product away, they go back to email chains, PDF invoices, and Excel spend tracking — they'd scream. That's a Vohra-style 40%+ answer waiting to happen. Volume is high (30+ charters/year per firm), spend is locked ($375K+/year), and the buyer has internal pain (CFO asking why charter spend is 20% over budget). This is where Mission Control actually fits.

**The problem:** you don't have Mission Control built. You have a brokerage wedge. An EA booking one charter through you doesn't experience the painkiller — they experience the same thing any other broker offers, just with a better UI. The "can't live without it" product is Mission Control, which is Month 6-9 of your original sequence. Surveying EAs on the brokerage alone will give you a false low score that doesn't reflect the real product.

**The case for post-raise founders (2-8 trips/year)**

This is your stated beachhead. They're findable (LinkedIn, Loud Hawk audience), they have the credit card out, and they experience the full product on trip #1 — transparent price (Charter Index), trust signal (Safety Brief), deposit protection, one-person-handles-everything. The survey question "how would you feel if you couldn't use OSA?" maps to a real experience they just had.

**The problem:** they're the "vitamin" segment by your own earlier analysis. If OSA disappears, they go to Paramount and tolerate a worse experience. They might answer "somewhat disappointed" at scale, not "very disappointed." You'd be measuring a segment that will *never* hit 40% no matter how much you iterate.

---

**My recommendation: post-raise founders, with one critical reframe.**

Here's why. Rachitsky's research says the journey to PMF starts by finding just one company to truly love your product. You can't find that one company if you can't transact with them yet. The EA/CFO segment is a *future* PMF target that requires a product you haven't built. The founder segment is the PMF target you can *measure against deal #1*.

The reframe: stop comparing founders to Paramount (which makes them a vitamin). Start comparing them to **Delta One + wasted travel days**, which was your own category positioning. A post-raise founder who used to lose two days flying commercial to a board meeting, now flying private for 60% more money but gaining a full workday back, with price transparency and zero sales calls — that's a painkiller. They just don't know it until they experience it.

Your earlier "vitamin" verdict assumed the competitive alternative was another broker. It isn't. It's their calendar.

So: design the PMF engine for **post-raise founders**, but write the survey in a way that surfaces the *right* competitive alternative in their answers. If the "main benefit" question comes back with "got my quote in 2 minutes instead of 6 hours," you're measuring against brokers (vitamin). If it comes back with "didn't lose a travel day on the way to my board meeting," you're measuring against Delta One (painkiller). Both answers will show up. Track the ratio.

Then, once you have 50+ deals closed and Mission Control v1 built, you run the EA/CFO survey as a *second* engine — that's the Month 9-12 milestone from your original sequence.

One engine now. Second engine later. Not both at once.
