# Initial client inquiry triage process

## Phelipe

When a client reaches out with a trip request — whether it's a call, a text, or an email — what's the first thing you're actually doing before you ever contact an operator? Walk me through what happens in your head and on your screen in the first 15 minutes after you receive a real inquiry.

---

## Claude

Good question. Here's what the first 15 minutes should look like, broken into the actual sequence of thinking and actions.

---

**ANSWER**

The first 15 minutes are about qualifying the client, locking down the trip parameters, and preparing to source — not contacting operators yet. You're building enough information to send a clean, professional quote request that gets fast responses.

---

**THE FIRST 15 MINUTES — STEP BY STEP**

**Minutes 0–2: Read and assess the inquiry.**
Before responding, you're answering these questions internally: Is this a real buyer or a price-shopper? Do I have enough detail to source, or do I need to ask follow-ups? Is this trip operationally feasible (timing, airports, passenger count)?

Red flags to note immediately: no name, vague dates ("sometime next month"), unrealistic budget expectations stated upfront, or requests that suggest they're collecting quotes for comparison with no intent to book.

**Minutes 2–5: Acknowledge the inquiry.**
Respond to the client. The goal is speed — you want to be the first broker who replies. Your acknowledgment should confirm you received the request, set a timeline for when they'll have options (typically 1–2 hours for domestic, longer for international), and ask only the missing critical details.

Critical details you need before sourcing:

- Departure city/airport (or general area)
- Destination city/airport
- Date and approximate departure time
- One-way or round trip (if round trip, return date/time)
- Passenger count
- Any pets, oversized luggage, or special requirements

That's it. Don't ask about budget, catering preferences, or aircraft type yet. Those come later. Asking about budget this early signals inexperience to a real buyer.

**Minutes 5–8: Log the deal in your CRM.**
Create the contact (if new) and create a deal record. At this stage the deal status is "Inquiry." Record the route, date, pax count, how they found you (referral source matters for tracking), and any notes about tone or urgency.

**Minutes 8–15: Pre-source assessment.**
This is what happens in your head before you email a single operator:

First, aircraft category. Based on pax count and route distance, you're mentally categorizing: 1–4 pax under 2 hours = light jet. 5–7 pax or 2–4 hours = midsize or super-mid. 8+ pax or 4+ hours = heavy jet. These are rough filters, not quotes.

Second, airport feasibility. Are there runway restrictions at either end? If they said "Aspen," you already know that's a restricted field — not every aircraft type can land there, and weather cancellations are common. If they said "Teterboro," you know slot availability can be tight. This is where your pilot background matters.

Third, operator shortlist. You're mentally (or in your Airtable) pulling up 3–5 operators who cover this route, have the right aircraft category, and have been reliable. You're thinking about who's likely to have positioning availability, who's responsive, and who gives you competitive pricing.

Fourth, timing realism. If the trip is in 6 hours, you know you're in "hot request" territory and need to move fast with operators you trust. If it's 3 weeks out, you have time to shop more carefully.

**METRICS TO TRACK**

- Time from inquiry received → first response (target: under 10 minutes during business hours)
- Percentage of inquiries with complete trip details on first contact vs. requiring follow-up
- Inquiry source (referral, website, cold outreach, social) for each deal

---

**RISKS / EDGE CASES**

- Client texts at 11 PM with a trip departing at 6 AM — you need a protocol for after-hours. Even a quick "Received, working on this now" buys trust.
- Client gives a city pair that's ambiguous (multiple airports). Don't assume — confirm. "For New York, do you have a preference between Teterboro, Westchester, or Republic?"
- Client says "just get me a price" with almost no details. Push back politely: you need at minimum the route, date, and pax count. Quoting without those wastes operator goodwill.

## Claude

**ANSWER**

The quote request to operators is a standardized, professional message that gives them everything they need to price the trip in one pass. When quotes come back, price is maybe 30% of the decision. The rest is aircraft condition, operator safety profile, crew quality, positioning logistics, and contract terms that protect your client if something goes wrong.

---

**PART 1: WHAT YOU SEND TO OPERATORS**

The sourcing request needs to be tight enough that the operator's charter sales desk can quote without calling you back for clarification. Every back-and-forth adds 20–60 minutes to your turnaround. One clean message, multiple operators, simultaneously.

**PASTE-READY: OPERATOR QUOTE REQUEST TEMPLATE**

> Subject: Charter Quote Request — [CITY] → [CITY] — [DATE]
>
> Hello [Charter Desk / Name],
>
> Requesting availability and pricing for the following:
>
> **Route:** [Departure airport/city] → [Arrival airport/city]
> **Date:** [Date]
> **Departure window:** [Time range, e.g., 10:00–12:00 local]
> **Passengers:** [Number]
> **Trip type:** [One-way / Round trip — if RT, include return date/time]
>
> **Aircraft category requested:** [Light / Midsize / Super-mid / Heavy — or "open to options"]
> **Special requirements:** [Pets, oversized luggage, catering level, Wi-Fi needed, etc. — or "None"]
>
> Please include in your quote:
> - Aircraft type and tail number (or series)
> - Year of manufacture and interior refurbishment year
> - Positioning details (where is the aircraft based)
> - Crew information if available
> - FET inclusion or exclusion
> - Cancellation terms
>
> Quote needed by: [Time/date]
>
> Thank you,
> [Your name]
> OSA Connect
> [Phone]

**Why each line matters:**

Asking for tail number or series tells the operator you're not a casual inquiry — you're going to vet the aircraft. This alone changes how seriously they treat your request.

Year and refurb year matters because a 2008 airframe with a 2022 interior is a completely different product than a 2008 airframe with the original cabin. Your client will notice.

Positioning details tell you where the aircraft is sitting right now. If your client departs Chicago Midway and the aircraft is based in Indianapolis, there's a 45-minute repositioning flight baked into the cost. If another operator has an aircraft already sitting at Midway, that positioning cost disappears and the trip is simpler operationally.

FET (Federal Excise Tax) — 7.5% on domestic charter — is sometimes included in the quote, sometimes not. If you're comparing two quotes and one includes FET and the other doesn't, you're not comparing the same number. Always confirm.

Cancellation terms vary wildly. Some operators give a full refund up to 72 hours before departure. Some lock you in at signing. This matters enormously to your client and to your liability.

---

**PART 2: WHAT YOU EVALUATE WHEN QUOTES COME BACK**

You'll typically get responses within 1–4 hours from good operators. Here's the actual evaluation framework, in the order I'd weight it.

**1. Safety and compliance (non-negotiable filter)**

Before anything else: Is this operator FAA Part 135 certificated? Do they hold an ARGUS Gold or Platinum rating, or Wyvern Wingman registration? If you don't know, you check before presenting the option to your client.

This isn't a marketing checkbox. ARGUS and Wyvern conduct independent audits of maintenance records, pilot training standards, insurance levels, and operational history. An operator with no third-party safety rating isn't necessarily unsafe, but you're taking on liability risk by recommending them without doing your own due diligence on their 135 certificate, insurance, and maintenance program.

If an operator can't provide proof of insurance with adequate liability coverage (industry standard minimum is typically $100M for light/mid jets, higher for heavy), that quote gets discarded regardless of price.

**2. Aircraft condition and vintage**

Two "Citation XLS+" quotes can represent completely different client experiences. You're evaluating: interior refurbishment year (how does the cabin actually look and feel), avionics suite (newer avionics often means smoother flight experience and better weather capability), and known mechanical reliability of the specific aircraft type.

A 2024 Challenger 350 with a fresh interior and a crew based at the departure airport is a fundamentally different product than a 2010 Challenger 300 repositioning from two states away with the original interior. The client may not know that, but you do.

**3. Positioning logistics**

This is where your pilot brain earns its value. If the aircraft has to reposition, you're thinking about: how far is the repositioning flight, does the repo flight add weather risk (the aircraft has to fly through a front to get to your client), does repo timing give enough buffer for delays, and is the operator padding the quote to cover repo fuel or are they eating it.

A more expensive quote from an operator with an aircraft already on-field at your departure airport is often the better recommendation because it eliminates an entire category of risk — the aircraft not showing up on time because the positioning leg had a problem.

**4. Crew quality**

This is harder to evaluate from a quote alone, but experienced brokers build knowledge over time. What you're looking for: total flight hours of the PIC (pilot in command), type-specific experience in that aircraft, and whether the crew is full-time for the operator or contract. Full-time crews generally mean higher training standards and better familiarity with the specific aircraft.

Some operators will provide crew bios if you ask. For high-value or complex trips, always ask.

**5. Operator responsiveness and professionalism**

This is a leading indicator of trip-day performance. If the operator took 6 hours to return a quote, gave incomplete information, or required three follow-ups to get basic details, that tells you something about how they'll perform when your client is standing on the ramp and something changes.

The operator who responds in 90 minutes with a clean, complete quote is signaling operational discipline. That's worth a price premium.

**6. Contract terms and cancellation policy**

You're comparing: cancellation window and refund terms, payment terms (deposit percentage, when final payment is due), what happens if the operator cancels or substitutes aircraft (do they guarantee a replacement of equal or better category), and liability allocation.

A cheaper quote with a "no refund after signing" policy is a worse deal for your client than a moderately more expensive quote with 48-hour cancellation flexibility — especially for trips booked more than a week out.

**7. Price**

Now you look at price. After everything above checks out.

Price variance of 5–15% between comparable options is normal and expected. The cheaper option is not automatically the better recommendation.

---

**PART 3: WHEN TO RECOMMEND THE MORE EXPENSIVE OPTION**

Concrete scenarios where you'd steer your client toward the higher quote:

**Scenario A — Positioning risk.** Cheaper quote requires a 2-hour repositioning flight. More expensive quote has the aircraft on-field. Client's trip is time-sensitive (morning meeting, connecting to a commercial international flight, event with a hard start time). You recommend the on-field aircraft because a repo delay cascades into a missed commitment.

**Scenario B — Aircraft quality gap.** Both aircraft are the same type, but the more expensive option was refurbished 18 months ago and the cheaper one has a 12-year-old interior. Your client is a first-time charter user or is flying with a principal whose experience matters for the relationship. You recommend the newer cabin.

**Scenario C — Operator track record.** You've used the cheaper operator before and experienced a last-minute aircraft substitution (they swapped the quoted aircraft for an older one). The more expensive operator has never substituted on you. You recommend reliability.

**Scenario D — Cancellation flexibility.** Client's trip depends on a deal closing. If the deal doesn't close, the trip doesn't happen. The cheaper quote is non-refundable. The more expensive quote allows cancellation with 48 hours notice. You recommend the flexible terms.

**Scenario E — Airport-specific operations.** The trip involves a challenging airport (Aspen, Eagle, Teterboro in winter, St. Barths). The cheaper operator's crew has limited experience at that field. The more expensive operator's crew has hundreds of landings there. You recommend the experienced crew because a diversion due to a missed approach costs your client far more than the price difference.

---

**HOW YOU PRESENT THIS TO THE CLIENT**

You never send raw operator quotes. You build a comparison that translates these factors into client-relevant language. The format should look something like:

> **Option A — [Aircraft type]**
> Operated by: [Operator name]
> Aircraft year / interior: [Year / refurb year]
> Safety rating: [ARGUS Gold / Wyvern Wingman / etc.]
> Positioning: [On-field at departure airport / repositioning from X]
> Cancellation: [Terms summary]
> Price: $[Amount] (includes FET)
>
> **Option B — [Aircraft type]**
> [Same structure]
>
> **My recommendation:** Option [X], because [one or two sentences — e.g., "the aircraft is already based at Midway, eliminating repositioning risk, and the operator has a strong track record on this route."]

That recommendation is where your value as a broker lives. Anyone can forward a quote. The broker who explains *why* one option is better earns repeat business.

---

**METRICS TO TRACK**

- Average number of operators contacted per trip (target: 3–5)
- Average time from quote request sent → quotes received
- Percentage of trips where the recommended option was accepted vs. client chose cheapest
- Operator substitution or cancellation rate (per operator — this builds your reliability database)

---

**RISKS / EDGE CASES**

- Operator quotes a "too good to be true" price significantly below market. This sometimes means an aircraft that's repositioning empty anyway (legitimate savings), but it can also mean an older aircraft, contract crew, or an operator trying to fill hours on a plane with maintenance coming due. Always verify.
- Client pushes back hard on price and demands the cheapest option. You present the risks clearly, document that you made a recommendation, and let them choose. Never overrule the client, but make sure your recommendation is on record.
- International trips add layers: overflight permits, customs/immigration coordination, cabotage rules, fuel surcharges, handler fees. Quote requests for international trips need to explicitly ask whether these are included.
- An operator responds with "aircraft subject to owner approval." This means the plane is a managed aircraft and the owner has the right to pull it. Flag this to your client as a scheduling risk.

## Claude

**ANSWER**

Aspen is the best example because it touches every variable at once — runway limitations, aircraft type restrictions, weather-driven cancellation risk, crew qualification requirements, and seasonal pricing spikes. A charter request to Aspen fundamentally changes the sourcing process compared to a routine city pair like Chicago to Miami.

---

**WHAT MAKES ASPEN DIFFERENT**

Aspen-Pitkin County Airport (ASE) sits at 7,820 feet elevation in a mountain valley. That single fact cascades into everything.

**Runway.** Aspen has one runway, approximately 8,000 feet long. At sea level, that's adequate for most charter aircraft. At nearly 8,000 feet elevation, air is thinner, engines produce less thrust, and wings generate less lift. The effective runway performance shrinks significantly. This means heavy jets — Gulfstream G-IV, Global Express, Falcon 7X — generally cannot operate into Aspen. Some super-midsize jets are restricted depending on temperature, passenger load, and fuel requirements. The aircraft that work reliably at Aspen are typically light jets, midsize jets, and select super-midsize types with the performance margins to handle the altitude.

So when a client says "I need a Gulfstream to Aspen," the answer is often no. Not because of availability or price, but because of physics. This is the first conversation a broker has to navigate — explaining why the aircraft type the client wants may not be the aircraft type that can safely operate into their destination.

**The alternative.** For clients who specifically want a heavy jet, the common workaround is flying into Eagle County Regional Airport (EGE), about 70 miles from Aspen. Eagle has a longer runway at a slightly lower elevation and can accept larger aircraft. But now you've added a 90-minute ground transfer to the trip. Some clients accept this. Some don't. Either way, the trip plan just changed because of the destination airport.

**Approach procedures.** Aspen's instrument approach has a higher-than-normal minimum descent altitude because of the surrounding terrain. In practical terms, this means the weather ceiling required for landing is much higher than at a flatland airport. At Chicago Midway, an aircraft might shoot an approach down to 200 feet above the runway. At Aspen, the minimum visibility and ceiling requirements are substantially higher. If the clouds are sitting in the valley — which happens frequently in winter — the aircraft cannot attempt the approach.

This creates a real operational risk: your client's aircraft departs Chicago in clear weather, flies two hours, arrives in the Aspen area, and cannot land because the valley is socked in. Now the crew has to divert — typically to Eagle, Rifle, or Grand Junction. Your client is on the ground at an airport they didn't intend to be at, needing ground transportation arranged on the spot.

**Crew qualifications.** Not every pilot can fly into Aspen. The FAA requires specific training and experience for Part 135 operations into ASE. Operators must have pilots who have completed approved mountain flying training, and many operators additionally require their pilots to have completed a minimum number of prior flights into Aspen specifically, often with a check airman.

When you send a quote request for an Aspen trip, one of your evaluation criteria becomes: does this operator regularly fly into ASE, and are their crews current on the approach? An operator who rarely goes to Aspen but has an available aircraft and competitive pricing is a worse option than an operator who flies that route weekly, even at a higher price. The crew familiarity with the terrain, the visual references on the approach, and the decision-making about when to attempt versus when to divert — that experience is worth real money.

**Noise abatement and curfew.** Aspen has strict noise restrictions. Certain older aircraft types are prohibited entirely. There's a voluntary nighttime noise curfew, and the airport authority monitors compliance closely. If your client wants a late arrival, this becomes a constraint. It also narrows the aircraft pool further — some aircraft types that could technically land at ASE from a performance standpoint are excluded because of noise category.

---

**HOW THIS CHANGES THE SOURCING PROCESS**

For a routine trip — say, Chicago Midway to Miami Opa-Locka — you send your standard quote request to 3–5 operators, get quotes back, compare on the factors we discussed, present options, done. The airports impose no special constraints.

For Aspen, the sourcing process changes at every step.

**Step 1 shifts from "which operators have aircraft" to "which operators are qualified for this airport."** You're filtering your operator list before you even send a request. Operators without Aspen-experienced crews get eliminated. This might cut your available pool by 40–60%, depending on the aircraft category.

**Step 2 adds aircraft performance verification.** You're not just looking for a midsize jet. You're confirming that the specific aircraft type can operate at ASE given the expected temperature on the travel date, the passenger count and baggage weight, and the fuel load required for the mission plus adequate reserves (including fuel to divert if the approach doesn't work). Hotter days at high altitude further degrade performance. A July trip to Aspen has different aircraft limitations than a January trip.

**Step 3 adds contingency planning.** For a Miami trip, you don't typically build a diversion plan into the client presentation. For Aspen, you should. The professional move is to proactively tell the client: "If weather prevents landing at Aspen, the crew will divert to Eagle. We'll have a ground transportation contact standing by as a contingency." This sets expectations and builds trust. The broker who doesn't mention this risk, and then the client gets diverted with no plan, loses that client permanently.

**Step 4 changes the pricing conversation.** Aspen trips are more expensive than the raw distance would suggest, for several reasons: the restricted operator and aircraft pool means less competition and higher pricing, positioning is often longer because fewer aircraft are based at ASE, the operational complexity commands a premium, and seasonal demand (ski season, roughly December through March) further inflates pricing. A light jet from Chicago to Aspen in February might run 25–40% higher than the same aircraft category to a comparable-distance flatland destination. The client needs to understand this isn't arbitrary markup — it's driven by the operational reality of the airport.

**Step 5 adds ground coordination.** Aspen's FBO (fixed-base operator — the private aviation terminal) gets extremely congested during peak season. Ramp space is limited. If your client's aircraft arrives and there's no ramp space, the aircraft may need to park at a remote location or even reposition to another airport to wait. During peak weekends (Christmas week, Presidents' Day), FBO slot reservations become necessary. This is a detail most clients have no idea about, and it's another thing the broker manages.

---

**WHAT THE CLIENT PRESENTATION LOOKS LIKE FOR AN ASPEN TRIP**

Instead of just presenting aircraft options and price, your quote package for a challenging airport should include:

**Aircraft option(s)** with the standard details — type, year, interior, operator, safety rating, price.

**Airport-specific notes:** runway and performance confirmation for the quoted aircraft, approach weather minimums explained in plain language ("If cloud cover drops below X, the crew will divert to Eagle County"), noise restrictions or curfew that affect the schedule.

**Contingency plan:** designated diversion airport, estimated ground transport time and cost from the diversion airport to the final destination, ground transport provider contact (pre-arranged, not scrambled after the fact).

**FBO and ground details:** which FBO at Aspen, ramp availability status during peak season, ground transportation at Aspen arranged or recommended.

This level of detail is what separates a broker who understands operations from one who's just forwarding quotes. The client reads this and immediately knows their trip is being managed by someone who has thought through the scenarios before they happen.

---

**OTHER AIRPORTS THAT TRIGGER SIMILAR LOGIC**

This isn't unique to Aspen. The same category of added complexity applies to: Teterboro (slot restrictions, congestion, mandatory noise abatement departures), Eagle/Vail (elevation and winter weather, though less restrictive than ASE), St. Barths (extremely short runway, only certain small aircraft types, requires pilot certification specific to that airport), Sun Valley (mountain terrain, similar crew qualification requirements), Telluride (highest commercial airport in North America, severe performance limitations), and any international destination requiring overflight permits, customs pre-clearance, or handler coordination.

Each of these changes the sourcing process, narrows the operator pool, adds variables to evaluate, and gives the broker an opportunity to demonstrate expertise that justifies their commission.

---

**METRICS TO TRACK**

- Percentage of trips involving operationally complex airports (helps you understand how much of your deal flow requires advanced sourcing)
- Diversion rate per airport (build this over time — it informs how you set client expectations)
- Client satisfaction on complex-airport trips vs. routine trips
- Whether contingency plans were activated and how they performed

---

**RISKS / EDGE CASES**

- Client insists on a heavy jet to Aspen despite your explanation. Document your recommendation clearly. If they push, offer the Eagle County alternative with ground transport as the heavy-jet-compatible solution. Never let a client pressure you into recommending an operation that's unsafe or infeasible.
- Operator says they can do Aspen but you discover their crews haven't flown there recently. Ask directly: how many times has this crew operated into ASE in the past 12 months? If the answer is vague, find another operator.
- Weather deteriorates between booking and trip day. Have the diversion plan ready before departure, not after the crew calls from the air saying they can't get in. The client should already know the backup plan exists.
- Peak season FBO congestion means the aircraft can't park. During Christmas week and major holiday weekends at Aspen, confirm FBO ramp reservations at the time of booking, not the day before.

## Claude

**ANSWER**

The client receives 2–3 options, presented in a single clean document or email, with enough detail to make a confident decision but not so much that it feels like homework. Every presentation ends with a clear recommendation and the reasoning behind it in plain language.

---

**WHY 2–3 OPTIONS, NOT MORE**

One option feels like a take-it-or-leave-it ultimatum. Four or more creates decision paralysis, especially for clients who don't know the differences between aircraft types. Two to three options gives the client a sense of choice and control while keeping the decision manageable.

The options should be meaningfully different from each other — not three versions of the same aircraft at slightly different prices. Ideally you're presenting across a dimension the client cares about: a price-conscious option vs. a premium option, or a category comparison (midsize vs. super-midsize), or a tradeoff between schedule flexibility and cost.

If you only received one viable quote after sourcing — which happens, especially for short-notice or complex trips — present it as one option with a clear explanation of why it's the right one. Don't manufacture fake alternatives to create the illusion of choice.

---

**WHAT THE CLIENT ACTUALLY RECEIVES**

The format depends on the client relationship and how the inquiry came in. For a first-time client or a formal corporate contact, send a PDF or well-formatted email. For an existing client who texts you, a clean text or WhatsApp message with the key details works — match the channel they used.

Regardless of format, every presentation contains the same core information.

**Header section:**
- Client name
- Trip summary (route, date, passengers)
- Date the quote was prepared
- Quote validity window (typically 24 hours — aircraft availability changes constantly)

**Per option:**
- Aircraft type and a brief plain-language description of the cabin
- Number of seats and baggage capacity
- Operator name (some brokers withhold this until booking — your call, but transparency builds trust)
- Safety rating
- Aircraft year and interior condition (year of last refurbishment)
- Where the aircraft is based (and whether it's on-field or repositioning)
- Estimated flight time
- Departure and arrival airports confirmed
- What's included (FET, fuel, crew, landing fees, ramp fees, segment fees, de-icing if applicable)
- What's NOT included (catering, ground transport, international fees if applicable)
- Cancellation terms (summarized in one line)
- Total price

**Recommendation section:**
- Which option you recommend and why, in 2–3 sentences of plain language

**Next steps:**
- What the client needs to do to confirm (typically: approve the option, sign the charter agreement, submit passenger names and payment)
- Your availability and direct contact for questions

**HOW YOU FRAME THE RECOMMENDATION**

The recommendation needs to do two things: state a clear choice and explain the reasoning in terms the client understands. Avoid aviation jargon. Translate everything into what it means for their experience.

**Example recommendation language by scenario:**

**When you're recommending based on positioning/reliability:**

> I'd recommend Option 2. The aircraft is based at Midway, so there's no repositioning flight required — it'll be on the ramp and ready when you arrive. Option 1 is priced lower, but the aircraft would need to fly in from St. Louis that morning, which introduces a timing variable we can avoid.

**When you're recommending based on aircraft quality:**

> I'd recommend Option 1. Both aircraft fly the same route in similar time, but the Citation Latitude was refurbished last year — newer cabin, updated seating, and Wi-Fi that works reliably. For a [first trip / client meeting / family trip], the cabin experience will be noticeably better.

**When you're recommending based on operator track record:**

> I'd recommend Option 2. We've worked with this operator on several trips and they've been consistently reliable — on time, clean aircraft, professional crew. Option 1 is a good aircraft, but we haven't flown with that operator before, and on a trip with a tight schedule I'd rather go with a team we know performs.

**When you're recommending based on cancellation flexibility:**

> I'd recommend Option 1. The pricing is slightly higher, but the cancellation terms give you full flexibility up to 48 hours before departure. Given that your schedule may shift, that flexibility is worth the difference. Option 2 is non-refundable once signed.

**When you're recommending based on airport-specific factors (Aspen, etc.):**

> I'd recommend Option 2. This crew flies into Aspen regularly — they know the approach and the weather patterns well. Option 1 is a strong operator, but their primary routes are Southeast domestic, and crew familiarity with mountain airports makes a meaningful difference in winter conditions.

**When the client should consider the cheaper option:**

> For this trip, Option 1 is the straightforward choice. Both operators are well-rated, and the aircraft are comparable in age and condition. Option 1 prices lower because the aircraft is already positioned closer to the departure airport. There's no compromise in going with the more efficient option here.

Notice that last one — you should recommend the cheaper option when it's genuinely the better or equivalent choice. If you always push the more expensive option, clients will eventually notice and trust erodes. The recommendation has to be honest every time or it has no value at all.

---

**WHAT TO LEAVE OUT OF THE CLIENT PRESENTATION**

Don't include your operator cost or margin. The client sees a total price. Your markup is between you and your accounting. Standard brokerage markup in the industry ranges from roughly 8–15% depending on trip size and relationship, but that's your business, not the client's.

Don't include raw operator quotes, internal notes, or back-channel details about why you rejected other options. The client doesn't need to know you received a fourth quote from an operator you passed on because their cancellation record is poor. They need the curated short list.

Don't include lengthy disclaimers or legal language in the presentation itself. That goes in the charter agreement, which is a separate document. The presentation is a sales and advisory tool — keep it clean.

Don't overwhelm with aviation specs. The client doesn't need thrust ratings, range charts, or MTOW figures. They need seat count, cabin size in human terms ("stand-up cabin" or "you'll need to duck slightly when boarding"), baggage capacity ("fits 4–5 full-size bags comfortably"), and flight time.

---

**TIMING AND FOLLOW-UP**

Send the presentation within the timeframe you promised in your initial acknowledgment — typically 1–2 hours for domestic, same day for complex or international. If you're going to miss that window, send a brief update explaining why and giving a revised ETA. Silence kills deals.

After sending, wait 2–4 hours and then follow up once by the same channel. If the client came in by email, follow up by email. If by text, follow up by text. The follow-up should be simple:

> Just checking that you received the flight options. Happy to jump on a call if you'd like to talk through them. The quotes are valid through [time/date] — after that I'd need to reconfirm availability.

That last line creates gentle urgency without pressure. It's also operationally true — aircraft availability changes, especially within 48–72 hours of a departure date.

If the client goes silent for more than 24 hours, one more follow-up is appropriate. After that, you log the deal as stalled in the CRM and move on. Chasing aggressively destroys the premium feel of the service.

**RISKS / EDGE CASES**

- Client shares your presentation with another broker to leverage pricing. This happens. It's why you don't include operator cost breakdowns. If you suspect it's happening, keep your service and expertise as the differentiator — the other broker can't replicate your judgment, just undercut the number.
- Client asks "can you do better on the price?" You can go back to the operator and negotiate, but do it selectively. If you renegotiate every deal, operators start quoting you higher to build in the expected discount. Better approach: negotiate hard during initial sourcing so the first quote you present is already competitive.
- Client wants to add passengers or change the date after you've presented. This isn't a minor edit — it may require re-sourcing entirely. Additional passengers can push you into a different aircraft category. A date change means re-checking availability from scratch. Acknowledge the change, set a new timeline, and re-source.
- Quote expires before the client decides. Don't honor expired quotes — reconfirm with the operator first. Aircraft may have been booked by someone else. Presenting an expired quote as still valid and then having to retract it is a serious trust violation.

**ANSWER**

The window between "I'll take option two" and the client stepping onto the aircraft is where most brokerage failures happen. The client thinks the trip is done. In reality, you're managing a coordination chain across 5–8 parties — operator, crew, FBO, ground transport, catering, and sometimes customs or security — where a single dropped detail can degrade the entire experience.

---

**THE FULL SEQUENCE: CLIENT CONFIRMS → WHEELS UP**

**PHASE 1 — CONTRACT AND PAYMENT (Hours 0–4 after confirmation)**

The moment the client says yes, you're doing three things simultaneously.

First, send the charter agreement. This is the legal contract between the client and OSA Connect (or between the client and the operator, depending on your brokerage structure — you need to have this figured out with your aviation attorney before your first deal). The agreement should already be templated from your lawyer. You're populating it with the trip-specific details: route, date, aircraft type and tail number, operator name, passenger count, total price, deposit and final payment amounts, cancellation policy, and liability terms.

The client signs and returns it. Electronic signature is fine and expected. Don't let this drag — aircraft availability is not guaranteed until the operator receives confirmation and deposit from you.

Second, collect the deposit. Standard practice is 50% at booking, balance due 48–72 hours before departure. Some operators require full payment upfront for short-notice trips. Wire transfer is the standard payment method for charter. Have your banking details ready in a clean, professional format so the client can initiate the wire immediately.

Third, confirm the booking with the operator. Once you have the signed agreement and deposit (or confirmation of wire initiation), you confirm the trip with the operator in writing. This is not a phone call — this is a written confirmation email that locks in the aircraft, crew, and schedule. The operator should respond confirming the booking. If they don't confirm in writing within a reasonable window, follow up immediately. Verbal confirmations are not confirmations.

**What can go wrong here:** The client delays signing or payment and the aircraft gets booked by someone else. This happens more often than you'd expect. When you present options, explain clearly that the aircraft is not held until the agreement is signed and deposit received. If the client needs 24 hours to decide, you can ask the operator to hold the aircraft — some will, some won't, and holds typically expire quickly.

---

**PHASE 2 — PASSENGER INFORMATION (24–72 hours before departure)**

You need to collect passenger details from the client. For domestic US flights, the minimum information required per passenger is full legal name (as it appears on government ID), date of birth, and weight (this matters for aircraft weight and balance calculations — it's not optional, it's a safety requirement).

For international flights, you additionally need passport number, nationality, passport expiration date, and potentially visa information depending on the destination.

Send the client a simple, clean form. Don't make them dig through an email chain to figure out what you need.

**PASTE-READY: PASSENGER INFORMATION REQUEST**

> [Client first name],
>
> To finalize your flight, I need the following for each passenger:
>
> - Full legal name (as shown on government-issued ID)
> - Date of birth
> - Approximate weight (required for aircraft weight and balance — this stays between us and the flight crew)
>
> [If international, add:]
> - Passport number
> - Nationality
> - Passport expiration date
>
> Please send this back at your earliest convenience, ideally by [date/time].
>
> Also — any catering preferences or requests? We can arrange anything from light snacks and beverages to a full meal service depending on the flight length.
>
> [Your name]
> OSA Connect

The weight question is awkward. Clients sometimes push back or provide obviously inaccurate numbers. Handle it matter-of-factly — the crew needs it for safety calculations, it's standard procedure, and the information is confidential. On light jets especially, weight and balance is a real operational constraint, not paperwork.

**What can go wrong here:** The client doesn't send passenger details until the last minute, or sends incomplete information. On international trips, discovering a passenger's passport is expired 12 hours before departure is a disaster you can't fix. Set a firm deadline and follow up proactively. Build a reminder at 72 hours, 48 hours, and 24 hours before departure if you still don't have complete information.

---

**PHASE 3 — OPERATIONAL COORDINATION (48–24 hours before departure)**

This is the phase the client never sees and where the broker earns their commission.

**Confirm with the operator — again.** Between booking and trip day, reconfirm the following with the operator's dispatch or charter sales desk: aircraft tail number (confirm it hasn't been substituted), crew assignment, departure time, FBO details at both departure and arrival airports, and any operational notes (weather, NOTAMs, runway closures, TFRs).

If the operator has substituted the aircraft since booking — which does happen, sometimes for legitimate maintenance reasons — you need to evaluate the substitute immediately. Is it the same category? Same or better vintage and interior? If the substitute is a downgrade, you have two options: push back on the operator and demand an equivalent replacement, or inform the client transparently and adjust pricing if the substitute is materially different. Never let a client show up to find a different aircraft than what they were promised without prior communication.

**Coordinate FBO arrangements.** Confirm the FBO (private terminal) at both ends. Provide the client with the FBO name, address, and phone number. At the departure end, confirm where the client should drive or be dropped off, where to park, and what to expect when they arrive ("Drive to Atlantic Aviation on the south side of the field. When you walk in, tell the front desk you're on the OSA Connect charter. They'll direct you to the ramp."). At the arrival end, confirm that the FBO is expecting the aircraft and that any ground services (fuel, ramp parking) are arranged.

This sounds like a small detail. It's not. A client who has never flown privately and drives to the wrong entrance at a large airport — or worse, drives to the commercial terminal — starts the experience confused and frustrated. Clear, specific arrival instructions eliminate that entirely.

**Arrange ground transportation.** If the client requested ground transport, confirm vehicles at both departure and arrival. Car service should be coordinated with the FBO so the driver knows exactly where to meet the client — at the FBO entrance or on the ramp, depending on the facility. Provide the client with the driver's name and phone number. Provide the driver with the client's name and flight details.

If the client is arranging their own ground transport, still send them the FBO address and any gate codes or access instructions needed to reach the private terminal.

**Arrange catering.** If the client requested catering, coordinate it through the operator or directly with the FBO's catering service. Confirm the order, delivery time (catering should be on the aircraft and set up before the client arrives), and any dietary restrictions. For short flights under 2 hours, light catering — beverages, snacks, maybe a charcuterie or fruit tray — is standard. For longer flights, a full meal can be arranged. Get the order confirmed in writing and confirm delivery timing with the caterer.

**What can go wrong here:** Aircraft substitution without notice, FBO miscommunication about arrival details, catering delivered late or incorrect, ground transport driver going to the wrong location. Each of these is preventable with written confirmations and redundant communication. The pattern is always the same: confirm in writing, confirm again closer to the date, and have a backup contact for every vendor in the chain.

**PHASE 5 — TRIP DAY (Morning of departure through wheels-up)**

Your job on trip day is monitoring, not relaxing.

**2–3 hours before departure:** Check weather at departure, arrival, and en route. If anything has changed that affects the flight, contact the operator to discuss and then update the client if needed. Confirm that catering has been delivered or is en route. Confirm ground transportation is on schedule.

**1 hour before departure:** The crew should be at the aircraft completing pre-flight. Confirm with the operator that the crew is on-site and the aircraft is ready. If there's any delay — maintenance issue, crew delay, late catering — you need to know now, not when the client arrives.

**When the client arrives at the FBO:** Ideally you've already spoken with the FBO front desk so they know to expect your client by name. The client should walk in, be greeted, and be escorted to the aircraft without confusion. If you're local and it's a high-value client or a first-time flyer, consider being at the FBO in person. That's not scalable long-term, but early on it makes an enormous impression.

**After departure:** The operator or FBO can usually confirm when the aircraft departs. Log the departure time. Monitor the flight if you have access to tracking (FlightAware, ForeFlight, or the operator's own tracking). When the aircraft lands, confirm arrival and make sure ground transportation at the destination connected with the client.

**What can go wrong here:** Mechanical issue discovered during pre-flight. Crew calls in sick. Weather deteriorates after the brief was sent. Catering never shows up. Client is late and the crew's duty time window starts closing. Each of these requires a real-time decision, and the client is looking to you — not the operator, not the FBO — to manage the situation.

The most important rule on trip day: **the client should hear about problems from you before they discover them.** If there's a 30-minute delay, you call the client before they're sitting in the FBO wondering why nothing is happening. If there's a mechanical issue and the aircraft needs to be swapped, you call the client with the situation and the solution at the same time. Never deliver a problem without a plan.

---

**PHASE 6 — POST-FLIGHT (Within 24 hours after landing)**

Send a brief follow-up. Thank the client, ask if everything met expectations, and note any feedback in the CRM. This was covered in your nurturing framework already, so I won't repeat it — but the key detail is that you also debrief internally. Log in your operator database: did the aircraft match what was quoted, was the crew professional, was the departure on time, were there any issues? This builds your institutional knowledge about which operators deliver and which ones don't.

---

**THE COORDINATION MAP — WHO YOU'RE MANAGING**

Between confirmation and wheels-up, you're coordinating across:

The operator's charter desk (booking confirmation, aircraft and crew status), the operator's dispatch (day-of operational updates), the departure FBO (client arrival, ramp handling, catering delivery), the arrival FBO (parking, ground services), the catering provider (order, delivery timing), the ground transportation provider at departure and arrival (vehicle, driver, timing, location), and the client themselves (passenger info, trip brief, day-of updates).

That's 6–8 separate points of contact for a single domestic one-way trip. A round trip doubles most of them. A multi-leg trip multiplies further. This is why organization and written confirmations matter — the broker who manages this on memory and phone calls eventually drops something.

- On-time departure rate (percentage of trips departing within 15 minutes of scheduled time)
- Aircraft substitution rate by operator
- Client satisfaction score or feedback per trip
- Number of trip-day issues per trip and resolution time
- Catering and ground transport execution accuracy

**RISKS / EDGE CASES**

- Mechanical issue grounds the aircraft 2 hours before departure. Immediately contact the operator for a replacement aircraft. Simultaneously start sourcing from your backup operators. Call the client with the situation and your recovery plan. Speed matters enormously — every minute of delay without communication amplifies the client's anxiety.
- Crew duty time limits are about to expire. Part 135 has strict crew rest and duty time regulations. If the trip is delayed long enough, the crew may "time out" and legally cannot fly. This is non-negotiable — it's a federal regulation. The solution is either a crew swap (if the operator has standby crews) or a departure delay until the crew is legal. Explain this to the client in safety terms, not bureaucratic terms.
- Client shows up with an extra passenger who wasn't on the manifest. This is not a minor issue. Weight and balance must be recalculated. The aircraft may not have enough seats or payload capacity. The passenger information needs to be collected on the spot. Handle it calmly, but don't let the flight depart without proper documentation.
- International trip and a passenger's passport is expired or missing. The flight can depart, but the passenger will be denied entry at the destination. There is no fix for this at the airport. This is why you set a hard deadline for passport information 48+ hours before departure.
- Client cancels day-of. Refer to the charter agreement cancellation terms. This is why the contract matters — it defines who absorbs the cost. Most day-of cancellations are non-refundable to the operator, which means they're non-refundable to the client. Deliver this news directly and factually, referencing the signed agreement.
