Here are your best bets in and around Wicker Park:

**Komorebi Sushi** (1324 N Milwaukee Ave) — This is the pick if you want a real Wicker Park sushi-focused dinner. 4.7 stars, creative rolls, fresh fish, intimate room. BYOB, which is either a bonus or a heads-up depending on your mood. Dinner only, opens 4 PM.

**Sushi Taku** (1904 W Division St) — The crowd favorite for volume. Solid AYCE option, almost always packed, reasonable prices, and open for lunch and dinner. Quality is good-not-great, but it's the best value play.

**Gorilla Sushi** (1238 N Milwaukee Ave) — Casual, cheap (~$4.49/plate), good for a quick lunch or takeout. Not destination sushi, but reliable.

If you're willing to go a few minutes out for a serious upgrade:

**Kai Zan** (2557 W Chicago Ave, ~10 min south) — Hands down the best omakase in the area. ~$$$ and reservations are tight (Wed–Sat only, closed Sun–Tue). This is the move for a special occasion.

Yes — though it was almost a decade ago, not a couple years.

Palantir filed suit against the U.S. Army in June 2016, after the Army issued a procurement solicitation for the next increment of its **Distributed Common Ground System–Army (DCGS-A2)** intelligence platform. The core argument: the Army wrote its requirements in a way that effectively shut out commercial off-the-shelf products like Palantir's, in violation of a 1994 statute (FASA § 2377) requiring the government to procure commercial items "to the maximum extent practicable."

A few notable beats:

- Palantir won at the Court of Federal Claims in October 2016, with the judge ruling on the FASA statutory violation rather than on bias/bad-faith grounds.
- The Federal Circuit upheld it on appeal in 2018 — the first time an appellate court forced the government to abide by the "commercial items to the maximum extent practicable" rule.
- In March 2019, the Army picked Palantir (alongside Raytheon) for the $876M DCGS-A successor contract — the company that sued them ended up winning the work.
- Since then, Palantir has racked up much bigger Army wins, including a $10B contract in 2025.

So the lawsuit was a foundational moment — it cracked open DoD procurement to Silicon Valley commercial software vendors, and it set up Palantir's entire defense-contracting trajectory.

## Claude

Not quite — the nuance matters here.

Palantir sued **before** any contract was awarded. It was a *pre-award bid protest*, meaning they challenged the structure of the solicitation itself, not a losing decision.

The sequence:

1. The Army issued a Request for Proposals (RFP) for DCGS-A2 that required **a single lead systems integrator** to build the platform from the ground up.
2. The way the RFP was written effectively excluded any company offering a pre-built commercial product — which is what Palantir sells. They couldn't even meaningfully bid.
3. Palantir argued this violated a 1994 federal law (FASA § 2377) requiring the government to consider commercial off-the-shelf products "to the maximum extent practicable" before commissioning custom-built ones.
4. They sued in June 2016 to *stop* the procurement, not to overturn an award.
5. The court agreed, enjoined the Army from awarding the contract under that solicitation, and forced them to redo it.
6. When the Army restructured the procurement, Palantir competed and won (alongside Raytheon) in 2019.

So the right framing: Palantir wasn't passed over for a contract — they were **structurally locked out of competing**, and they sued to force the Army to let commercial vendors bid at all. The lawsuit was about the *rules of the game*, not the outcome.
