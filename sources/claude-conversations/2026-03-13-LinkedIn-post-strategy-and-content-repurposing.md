Yes — that risk is real.

The exact nightmare is usually not “the customer defaulted” in the ordinary invoice sense. It is this: the broker takes a card, the operator wants cleared money before wheels-up, the broker pays or commits to pay the operator, and then the card payment is disputed, reversed, frozen, or held in reserve later. DOT rules also require brokers to make prompt refunds when refunds are due, including for credit-card purchases, which means the broker can get squeezed from both sides at once. ([Legal Information Institute][1])

That is one reason many charter terms lean toward bank wire for the main payment and use the card more as backup or for ancillaries. Air Integrity requires full wire 7 days out for round trips and immediate full payment by wire for bookings within 7 days, while keeping a card on file; Charter Flight Group also requires advance payment and allows a cash discount for wire payments. ([Air Integrity][2])

There are also real examples showing the pattern is not imaginary. Monarch Air Group alleged in a 2025 lawsuit that it secured aircraft and crew, then after cancellation it charged the counterparty’s card and the counterparty allegedly filed a false chargeback. Charter Flight Group was hit with a judgment tied to a flight that allegedly never happened and money that allegedly was not fully refunded. In another case, a private charter broker was criminally charged after allegedly pushing unauthorized card transactions and fake authorization codes through merchants. ([Access Newswire][3])

What breaks brokers is usually a short list of repeat mistakes:

1. **Using cards as primary funding for future travel without enough capital behind them.**
   Future-travel merchants are exposed because disputes can arrive long after the service date logic begins, and payment processors may hold reserves specifically to cover refunds and chargebacks. In plain English: the money looks like yours until suddenly it isn’t. ([Adyen][4])

2. **Paying the operator before the client funds are truly safe.**
   If you are the merchant of record on the card and you forward funds to the operator before the transaction is past dispute risk or otherwise adequately protected, you have created negative optionality for yourself. Your own OSA operating stack is already leaning the safer way: the newer Operating System says OSA does not hold client deposits and that deposits go directly to the operator, while the Day 1–3 plan defaults to bank wire and 100% upfront at booking.  

3. **Sloppy disclosures and contract timing.**
   Under Part 295, brokers have to disclose the operator, their role, total cost, and certain other items; if required disclosures are not made within a reasonable time, the charterer must get the opportunity to cancel and receive a full refund. That means a broker can lose not just goodwill but the legal footing for keeping the money. ([Legal Information Institute][5])

4. **Acting or advertising like the operator.**
   Part 295 specifically treats it as unfair or deceptive if a broker makes the public think the broker is the direct air carrier, or misrepresents the aircraft, service, timing, or safety record. NBAA guidance also warns brokers not to talk like “our aircraft” or “our flight” when they are not the operator. That kind of language feels slick until it becomes exhibit A. ([Legal Information Institute][6])

5. **Passing through surprise charges after the fact or quoting fuzzy totals.**
   NBAA’s Part 295 guidance flags unexpected post-flight bills as both a compliance issue and a contract issue. If de-ice, cleaning, Wi-Fi, ramp, or catering charges are not clearly handled in advance, that is fertile ground for disputes and chargebacks. ([NBAA][7])

6. **Weak recordkeeping.**
   When a chargeback hits, the broker needs one clean package: signed agreement, payment timeline, required disclosures, operator confirmation, passenger manifest details, communications, and proof of service. Your OSA docs are right to treat the CRM as an audit trail in case of dispute. Without that paper trail, “but everybody knew what was happening” becomes expensive poetry. ([Worldpay Support][8])

7. **Undercapitalization combined with selling too much certainty too early.**
   AeroVanti’s 2025 indictment involved large upfront member payments allegedly tied to promised aircraft access and asset-related promises that were not carried out as represented. Different model, same lesson: if cash from customers is being used to fund obligations ahead of actual delivery capacity, the business can tip from growth story to wreckage very fast. ([DOT OIG][9])

For OSA specifically, the safest launch posture based on both industry evidence and your own docs is:

* **Wire-only for core charter payments at launch.**
* **Do not let OSA be the soft underbelly between the client’s card dispute rights and the operator’s hard payment deadline.**
* **Either payment goes directly to the operator or, if OSA ever touches funds, only do so with counsel-approved structure and enough reserve to survive refunds and disputes.**

**Never pay irreversible supplier money with reversible client money.**

That one sentence prevents a lot of founder character development.
