## The federal layer (works the same everywhere, but Chicago numbers make it bigger)

**Depreciation is the headline.** Residential rental depreciates over 27.5 years, straight line. On a $725K Avondale 2-flat with ~$580K building basis (after stripping land), that's ~$21K/year of paper losses against rental income. **Cost segregation is where it gets aggressive.** A cost seg study on a $725K Chicago 2-flat reclassifies ~20–30% of the building basis into 5, 7, and 15-year property (appliances, flooring, fixtures, parking lot, landscaping). With **100% bonus depreciation now permanent under OBBBA 2025** (which you already know from the OSA Aircraft thesis), you can front-load $120–180K of depreciation into Year 1 on a single Chicago 2-flat purchase.

That's the move: buy a Chicago 2-flat in December, run cost seg, take ~$150K of bonus depreciation against your taxable income that same year. Cost of the cost seg study: $3–5K. ROI is absurd.

**Real Estate Professional Status (REPS) is the unlock.** This is the federal regime that converts paper losses from passive (limited deduction) to active (unlimited deduction against your W-2 or K-1). To qualify: 750+ hours/year in real property trades, and more than half your working hours in RE.

**You probably can't get REPS as a SkyWest pilot.** 750 hours is doable, but "more than half your working hours" is the killer when you're flying full schedule. However:

1. **Your future spouse can qualify.** REPS is per-spouse on a joint return, and the deductions flow to both. This is the standard high-income RE play.
2. **Post-cockpit (Year 11–12 in your plan), you qualify easily.** 750 hours managing a 4–6 property portfolio plus OSA Aircraft work that touches real property is plausible.

## The Illinois state layer

Illinois has a flat 4.95% income tax. Rental income flows through to your personal return at this rate. That's higher than Texas or Florida (zero) but lower than California (13.3%). Combined federal + state on RE cash flow lands around 28–32% nominal, but depreciation and cost seg drop the effective rate to 15–22%.

## The Cook County property tax problem (the big one)

This is the single biggest Chicago-specific tax issue and it'll burn you if you don't underwrite for it.

**Cook County reassesses on sale.** When you buy, the assessor unsticks the seller's frozen number. Your tax bill in Year 1 of ownership is often 30–60% higher than what the seller was paying. The property tax line in any pro forma you receive is almost always wrong — sellers and listing brokers show you the seller's bill, not yours.

**How to underwrite:** Estimate the new tax bill at roughly 1.8–2.2% of your purchase price for Chicago city properties, 2.0–2.5% in collar counties (DuPage, Lake, Will). On a $725K 2-flat, budget $13–16K/year, not the $7K the seller was paying.

**The appeal game is real.** Cook County's appeal system is one of the most active in the country. Every property owner should appeal every triennial reassessment. Specialized firms like Kensington Research or Saranow Law take 25–33% of first-year savings on contingency — so it costs you nothing to try. Average successful appeal in Cook County reduces assessed value 8–15%. Build this into your annual operating cadence.

## The 1031 exchange — Chicago-specific notes

1031s work the same federally regardless of state, but Illinois doesn't recognize 1031 deferral on the state portion in some interpretations — though in practice, Illinois follows federal treatment for personal income tax purposes on like-kind real property exchanges. Your CPA will confirm this, but functionally Chicago 1031s defer both federal capital gains and Illinois income tax on the gain.

**The strategic 1031 sequence for your plan:**

1. Phase 1 → Phase 3: Sell three or four 2–4 unit buildings, 1031 into one 10–20 unit building. Defer all gains.
2. Phase 3 → Phase 4: 1031 the leveraged 10-unit building into a smaller debt-free building. Defer again.
3. **Hold the final property until death.** Stepped-up basis at death wipes out all deferred gains. This is the "swap till you drop" strategy and it's the cleanest exit.

## The Chicago-specific structuring layer

**Land trusts.** Illinois has the strongest land trust statute in the country. Hold each property in an Illinois land trust with an LLC as beneficiary. The land trust gives you privacy (your name doesn't appear in public records — only the trustee's), and the LLC gives you liability protection. This is standard practice for any serious Chicago RE investor and it's a Chicago/Illinois-specific tool that doesn't exist this cleanly in most other states.

**Series LLCs.** Illinois allows series LLCs, which let you put each property in a separate "cell" under one master LLC. Lower formation cost than separate LLCs, same liability isolation. Worth using once you have 3+ properties.

**The tax stack at scale:**
- Each property in an Illinois land trust
- Beneficiary is a series LLC cell
- Master LLC is owned by you (or a holding entity)
- Holding entity might be the same Delaware C-Corp structure or a separate LLC depending on whether you want OSA and RE under one umbrella

Yes — Cook County reassessment is the biggest Chicago-specific tax trap and the one most pro formas get wrong. Here's the full picture.

## How Cook County reassessment actually works

Cook County reassesses every property on a **triennial cycle** — every three years, on a rotating schedule by region:

- **City of Chicago:** reassessed in years ending in 0, 3, 6, 9 (next: 2026 — happening now)
- **North suburbs:** years ending in 1, 4, 7
- **South/West suburbs:** years ending in 2, 5, 8

Between reassessments, your assessed value is "frozen" — it doesn't change with the market. This is why a long-term Chicago owner can be paying property taxes based on a 2017 valuation in 2026, while the building's market value has doubled.

## The "unsticking" event — why your Year 1 tax bill jumps

Here's the trap: **reassessment is also triggered by sale.** When you buy, the Cook County Assessor doesn't have to wait for the triennial cycle — they can (and usually do) reassess the property based on your purchase price, because the sale established a new market value.

This is the "unsticking." The seller's frozen assessment from 2017–2020 disappears, and your assessment resets to roughly your purchase price. The tax bill that follows can be 30–60% higher than what the seller was paying.

**Real example, Avondale 2-flat:**
- Seller's 2025 tax bill: $7,200/year (based on 2020 assessment of ~$420K)
- You buy for $725K in 2026
- Assessor resets assessed value to ~$725K market value (10% assessment ratio for residential = $72,500 assessed value)
- New tax bill Year 1: ~$13,500–15,000/year
- **Increase: ~$6–8K/year, every year, forever**

The listing broker showed you the $7,200 number. Your real number is double.

## The math behind the bill

Cook County property tax = Assessed Value × State Equalizer × Local Tax Rate − Exemptions

For Chicago residential:
- **Assessment ratio:** 10% of fair market value (residential under 7 units)
- **State equalizer:** ~3.0x (changes annually, smooths assessments across counties)
- **Composite tax rate:** ~7–8% in Chicago, varies by ward
- **Effective rate:** roughly 1.8–2.2% of fair market value annually for Chicago residential

For Chicago commercial (7+ units):
- **Assessment ratio:** 25% of fair market value
- Same equalizer and rate logic
- **Effective rate:** roughly 4.0–5.5% of fair market value annually

**This is why the 2–4 unit residential play is dramatically more tax-efficient than buying a 10-unit building.** The moment you cross the 7-unit threshold, your assessment ratio jumps from 10% to 25% — your property taxes effectively 2.5x. This is a Chicago/Cook County structural feature you have to underwrite around.

## What this means for your plan structure

This changes the Phase 3 consolidation strategy I described earlier. Going from four 2–4 unit buildings to one 10–20 unit building isn't just an operations decision — it's a tax structure decision.

**Option A: Stay residential at scale.** Hold six to eight 2–4 unit buildings instead of consolidating into one larger commercial property. Higher operational complexity, but you keep the 10% assessment ratio. On $4M of asset value, you save $80–120K/year in property taxes vs. the same value held as a single 10-unit commercial building.

**Option B: Consolidate to commercial, accept the tax hit.** Simpler operations, professional management economics work, but property taxes 2.5x. Only makes sense if the operational savings exceed $80–120K/year, which they don't at this scale.

**The actual answer for your plan: stay residential 2–4 unit through Phase 4.** Don't cross the 7-unit threshold per building. End state isn't "one 15-unit debt-free building" — it's "five or six 2–4 unit debt-free buildings" producing the same NOI but sheltered from the commercial assessment regime.

This is a meaningful revision to what I said earlier. The Phase 3 consolidation logic I gave you applies to most US markets. **In Cook County specifically, it's the wrong move because of the 10%/25% assessment cliff.**

## The appeal game

Reassessment is appealable, and the appeal system is genuinely active in Cook County. Three levels:

1. **Cook County Assessor's Office:** First appeal, free, can do yourself or with help.
2. **Cook County Board of Review:** Second appeal if assessor denies. Free.
3. **Illinois Property Tax Appeal Board (PTAB) or Circuit Court:** Final appeal, requires legal representation.

## Two separate mechanisms — they're not the same

**Mechanism 1: Reassessment (every 3 years).**
Your assessed value gets reset based on market value. This is the big jump. Between reassessments, your assessed value is frozen — it does NOT increase year by year.

**Mechanism 2: Tax rate changes (every year).**

So your tax bill can move every year — but not because your assessment moved. It moves because the **rate applied to your frozen assessment** changed.

## What that actually looks like

Year 1 (purchase year): Big reset. Assessment goes from seller's frozen number to ~your purchase price. Tax bill jumps 30–60%.

Years 2 and 3: Assessed value frozen. Tax bill moves only with rate/equalizer changes. Historically that's been 1–3% per year, sometimes flat, occasionally negative.

Year 4 (next triennial reassessment): If Chicago property values rose 15% since Year 1, your assessment jumps ~15%. Bill jumps accordingly.

Years 5 and 6: Frozen again. Small annual movement from rates only.

Year 7: Reassessment again. Etc.

- Year 4: Step up 10–20% (reassessment)
- Years 5–6: Flat with 1–2% rate creep

**Path B: Hold rent flat, recapture at turnover.**

- Higher single-event vacancy, lower ongoing friction

- 3% annual increase on existing tenants (below-market, retention-friendly)
- Reset to full market rent on every turnover
- Net effective rent growth across the portfolio: ~4–5% per year
- Lowest tenant friction, captures market growth via turnover

## The Illinois RE broker license process

Illinois changed its licensing structure in 2018 — they eliminated the "salesperson" license and made everyone start as a "broker." So when you hear "Illinois RE broker license," that's the entry-level license, not a senior credential.

**The actual requirements:**

**1. Pre-licensing education: 75 hours**
- 60 hours of general real estate principles (online or in-person)
- 15 hours of "interactive" coursework (live sessions, role-plays, case studies)
- Cost: $400-700 depending on provider (Real Estate Express, Kaplan, CE Shop are the main ones)
- Time: most people complete in 4-8 weeks at 10 hours/week

**2. Pass the state exam**
- Two parts: National (100 questions) + Illinois state law (40 questions)
- Passing score: 75% on each part
- Cost: $58 per attempt
- Pass rate: ~60-65% on first try (so about 1/3 of people fail and retake)

**3. Get sponsored by a managing broker**
- This is the catch most people don't expect
- You can't activate your license as a solo operator
- You must be sponsored by a licensed Illinois "Managing Broker" (the senior credential)
- Most new licensees join an established brokerage (Coldwell Banker, @properties, Compass, Baird & Warner) — they sponsor your license in exchange for a commission split
- Solo brokerages exist but require the Managing Broker tier

**4. Pay licensing fees**
- Initial license: $125
- Continuing education: 12 hours every 2 years ($100-200)
- Renewal: $150 every 2 years

**5. Background check + fingerprinting**
- ~$60
- Standard processing

**Total time: 8-12 weeks from start to active license**
**Total cost: ~$700-1,000**

## The sponsorship requirement is the real friction

This is what makes the Illinois RE license trickier than it looks on paper. As a new licensee, you're legally required to operate under a Managing Broker's supervision. You have three options:

**Option A: Join a traditional brokerage (Coldwell Banker, @properties, etc.)**
- They sponsor your license
- You get desk fees, training, MLS access, brand
- You give up 30-50% of commissions to the brokerage
- Most common path

**Option B: Join a "100% commission" brokerage (eXp, Realty One Group, etc.)**
- They sponsor your license
- You pay flat monthly fees ($85-150/month) instead of commission split
- Better for high-volume agents
- Less hand-holding, more independent

**Option C: Get the Managing Broker credential yourself**
- Requires 2 years as an active broker first
- Additional 45 hours of education
- Separate exam
- Then you can sponsor yourself
- Realistic timeline: 2.5-3 years from start

For your purposes, **Option B (100% commission brokerage) is probably the right path**. You're not trying to build a real estate career — you're trying to save commission on your own Chicago acquisitions and get MLS access. eXp Realty or similar lets you operate cheaply while staying compliant.
