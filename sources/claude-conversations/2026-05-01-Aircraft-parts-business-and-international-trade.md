# Aircraft Parts Business Rundown

## Market size and structure

The aircraft parts world splits into two very different businesses. **OEM new-build parts manufacturing** is roughly a $1.02 trillion market in 2025, projected to reach $1.39 trillion by 2030 — that's parts going into new aircraft on the assembly line. **Aftermarket parts** (replacement parts for the existing fleet) is the smaller but more profitable game at $54.4 billion in 2025, projected to hit $93.5 billion by 2032 at 8% CAGR.

Within aftermarket, there are three supply categories that matter:

- **OEM parts** — the dominant segment at $37.9 billion in 2024, sold by the original manufacturer (or their licensed channel). High margin, full warranty, no certification fights.
- **PMA (Parts Manufacturer Approval)** — third-party FAA-certified equivalents, the fastest-growing segment at 11.2% CAGR. Cheaper than OEM, same airworthiness.
- **USM (Used Serviceable Material)** — parts harvested from retired aircraft, inspected, recertified, resold. Growing fast for cost and sustainability reasons.

## The biggest players

**OEM-side giants** (they make parts for new aircraft and dominate aftermarket because they own the IP):
- **GE Aerospace, RTX (Pratt & Whitney + Collins Aerospace), Honeywell, Safran, Rolls-Royce** — engines and major systems.
- **Boeing** and **Airbus** — through their parts arms. Airbus owns **Satair**, which distributes OEM aircraft parts nose-to-tail across more than 300 manufacturers. Boeing's equivalent is **Aviall** (now folded into Boeing Distribution).

**Independent distributors and aftermarket specialists** (this is where the interesting money is):
- **HEICO** (NYSE: HEI) — the king of PMA. Holds 19,000+ FAA approvals, produces 500+ engineered parts annually, and services 32,000+ aircraft accessory components. They undercut OEM pricing 20–40% on parts they reverse-engineer. Trades at a premium multiple because of how durable the cash flow is.
- **AAR Corp** (NYSE: AIR) — stocks over one million items from more than 250 manufacturers, including airframe rotables, landing gear, APUs, LRUs.
- **Proponent** — world's largest independent aircraft parts distribution company, employee-owned, headquartered in Brea, CA.
- **VSE Aviation / Kellstrom Aerospace** — distribution, leasing, USM.
- **AJW Group, Werner Aero, Unical** — specialists in airline aftermarket and USM.

## How they actually make money

The business model varies by player:

The OEMs make most of their money on the *aftermarket lock-in*, not the initial sale. Engines especially — GE and Pratt sell engines at break-even or a loss, then earn 70–80% of lifetime value through long-term service agreements (LTSAs) and proprietary spare parts. This is why aftermarket margins are so high: high-value components like propulsion and avionics deliver gross margins 2–3x higher than standard structural components.

PMA shops like HEICO play a different game — they identify the highest-volume, highest-margin OEM parts, reverse-engineer them, get FAA-PMA approval, and sell at a discount to airlines who are sick of OEM monopoly pricing. Their moat is the *certification work* itself: each PMA approval takes years and hundreds of thousands of dollars in engineering and testing.

Distributors like Proponent and AAR make money on **inventory positioning and AOG response**. When an aircraft is grounded (Aircraft On Ground), a $5,000 part can be worth $50,000 to the operator because every hour costs them money. Distributors stock globally, ship in hours, and capture the urgency premium. They also do **consignment** (managing other people's inventory), **leasing** (renting parts while originals are repaired), and **teardowns** (buying retired aircraft and harvesting components for USM).

## Selling to Brazil specifically

The Brazil trade is large and structurally favorable to US suppliers right now. Aerospace products and parts are the largest source of bilateral trade between Brazil and the United States ($9.05B), and the largest sector for U.S. exports to Brazil. U.S. companies exported $7.25B in 2023, an increase of 6.5% from 2022. Brazil is the 6th largest export market for U.S. aerospace products.

Why the trade flows the way it does:

**Embraer is the anchor.** Embraer's industry supplies only around 20–25% of its needs. The non-produced parts are imported, which makes Embraer a huge buyer. Avionics, engines (P&W, GE), landing gear, hydraulics, fasteners, composites — all imported, mostly from US and European suppliers.

**The fleet is old and parts-hungry.** More than 40 percent of the general aviation fleet is composed of one or two-engine piston-powered aircraft with more than 30 years of use. The average age of the general aviation fleet is currently 30 years. Old fleets need a constant supply of replacement parts.

**Recent regulatory shift removed a big tax barrier.** In 2023, Brazil joined the World Trade Organization's Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft. This process of accessions seeks to ensure that import taxes on aircraft and aircraft parts are consolidated at zero, should bring predictability to input prices, and acts as a positive signal for potential investment. Before this, Brazilian importers paid stacked taxes (II + IPI + PIS/COFINS + ICMS) that could push effective duty to 30%+ on some HTS codes. Now civil aviation parts trend toward duty-free.

**ANAC is the gatekeeper.** Each product exported to Brazil must be accompanied by an export airworthiness approval (export airworthiness certificate, authorized release certificate or equivalent documents). For US-origin parts, that's an FAA 8130-3 tag. The US-Brazil Bilateral Aviation Safety Agreement (BASA) lets ANAC accept FAA certification with limited additional review, which is why US suppliers have an advantage over, say, Asian PMA shops.

**Distribution requires a local presence.** To succeed in Brazil, U.S. companies must either establish themselves in the country or have a well-informed local representative. It is important to have a distributor or systems integrator that can offer post-sale maintenance services, replacement parts, and repairs. Aircraft part suppliers without local representation may find it difficult to build relationships, get timely information, and gain access to procurement decision-makers.

## How the money flow works on a typical Brazil sale

A US distributor (say, Proponent or AAR) selling a $20K avionics LRU to a Brazilian operator:

1. Order placed via local rep or directly. Quote in USD.
2. Part shipped from US warehouse (Brea, Miami, Greensboro) with FAA 8130-3 tag attached.
3. Customs clearance in Brazil — Receita Federal verifies HTS code, ANAC verifies airworthiness documentation. Under the 2023 WTO accession, civil aviation HTS codes now generally clear at 0% II.
4. ICMS (state tax) and PIS/COFINS may still apply depending on structure — many operators use **RECOF-Sped** or **Drawback** regimes to defer or eliminate these on parts that go into MRO operations.
5. Distributor's gross margin on the sale: typically 25–40% on OEM parts they hold inventory on, higher on AOG, and 40–60%+ on PMA equivalents.

The structural reason US distributors dominate Brazil traffic is the combination of FAA/ANAC bilateral recognition, USD-denominated trade (which Brazilian operators are used to for aviation), Miami being the natural air-cargo hub for Latin America, and the fact that Embraer's own supply chain is deeply Americanized.

Good question. The honest answer is the inventory model is a capital trap and the brokerage model is where most independent operators actually make money — but the real edge is something different from both. Let me break down how the business actually works at the operator level.

## The two models, brutally

**Inventory model (stocking distributor)**
You buy parts, hold them, sell when needed. Examples: Proponent, AAR, Satair.

The math is rough for a new entrant:
- A serious rotables inventory for a single aircraft type (say E175) is $5–20M minimum to be credible
- Inventory turns in this business are slow — 1.5–3x per year is typical, vs 8–12x in normal distribution
- You're competing with 30-year-old players who have OEM authorizations you cannot get
- OEMs control allocation. HEICO and AAR can buy direct because of volume. You'd buy through them, marking up their markup.
- Carrying cost, obsolescence risk (a part for a retired aircraft is scrap), and warehouse + AS9120 certification overhead

This is a capital-intensive, low-margin grind unless you have a specific niche where you can dominate inventory.

**Brokerage model (on-call sourcing, no inventory)**
You don't own anything. Customer calls with a need, you work the phones, source from distributors/airlines/teardown shops, mark up 10–25%, ship direct or through your name.

The math:
- Near-zero capital required
- You make money on information asymmetry and AOG urgency
- Margins are thinner per transaction but ROIC is infinite (no capital tied up)
- The whole industry runs on **ILS (Inventory Locator Service)** and **PartsBase** — subscription databases where every distributor, airline, and broker lists what they have. A broker pays $5–15K/year for access and works the network.
- Reputation and response speed are the entire moat

This is how most small-to-mid aviation parts businesses actually start and survive. It's also brutally commoditized — there are thousands of brokers on ILS, all looking at the same listings.

## Where the real money is (and why neither pure model wins)

The actual successful independents do a **hybrid + specialization** play. Three patterns work:

**1. Niche inventory dominance.** Pick one aircraft family or one component category and own the inventory position. Example: a shop that holds the deepest inventory of King Air 350 landing gear components in the Americas. Customers call you first because nobody else has it. Capital required but defensible.

**2. Teardown/USM origination.** Buy retired aircraft, harvest parts, recertify, sell. AAR does this at scale. The margin is huge because you bought the airframe for scrap value and each rotable component sells for thousands. Requires capital ($2–10M per teardown) plus repair station relationships, but the gross margins are 60–80%+ on parts that move.

**3. Consignment + brokerage hybrid.** You don't buy inventory — you *manage* other people's inventory. Airlines and lessors have warehouses full of surplus parts they don't want to sit on. You take it on consignment, list it, sell it, split the proceeds (typically 70/30 to the owner). You get inventory depth without the capital. This is what Kellstrom and many mid-tier players actually do.

## The Brazil-specific angle

This is where it gets interesting for OSA-adjacent positioning, because the Brazil market has a structural gap most US distributors don't serve well.

**The gap:** Brazilian operators (especially smaller Part 135 equivalents, ag aviation, helicopter operators, and the long tail of GA) struggle to source parts efficiently. The big US distributors all have local reps, but those reps are focused on Embraer, Azul, GOL, LATAM. The mid-market and below gets terrible service — long lead times, no local stock, painful customs, language barriers, USD/BRL friction.

**The opportunity for a US-based operation:**
- Be the **specialized broker/distributor for Brazilian operators**, with the documentation, customs, and tax expertise as your real product
- Hold a small strategic inventory of high-velocity consumables for common Brazilian fleets (Embraer regional, Cessna Caravan, Bell helicopters, PT6 engine parts)
- Run the brokerage layer for everything else
- Charge a premium for end-to-end: USD invoicing, FAA 8130-3, customs clearance, RECOF-Sped structuring, ICMS optimization, BRL settlement option, Portuguese support
- Target: ag aviation, executive aviation maintenance shops, smaller scheduled operators, helicopter operators

**The real moat isn't parts — it's the trade infrastructure.** A Brazilian Part 145 shop doesn't want to deal with US distributors directly. They want one phone number, one invoice, parts in São Paulo cleared and ready, and predictable pricing. The parts are the commodity. The cross-border execution is the product.

## How I'd actually structure it

If I were building this from scratch with limited capital, the staged path is:

**Stage 1 — Pure brokerage with positioning (Year 1, ~$50–150K)**
- Form Delaware LLC, get DUNS, register on ILS and PartsBase
- AS9120 certification ($15–30K, takes 6–9 months)
- Build relationships with 20–30 Brazilian MROs and operators (this is where Embraer/SkyWest network and Portuguese-native advantage compound)
- Run as broker only — quote, source, ship, invoice. No inventory.
- Goal: prove the demand signal and learn the actual pain points

**Stage 2 — Consignment layer (Year 2, ~$100–300K working capital)**
- Take consignment from US airlines/lessors who want exposure to Brazilian buyers
- Stand up a small US warehouse (or 3PL through someone like Crane Worldwide in Miami)
- Add a Brazilian customs/forwarding partner — this becomes a co-branded service

**Stage 3 — Strategic inventory + Brazilian entity (Year 3+, $1–5M)**
- Open Brazilian Ltda or use bonded warehouse structure
- Hold 30–60 days of high-velocity SKUs in Brazil under RECOF-Sped or similar
- This is where you start beating incumbents on lead time

The capital ramp is staged so you don't bet $5M on day one. Each stage validates the next.

## What already exists at the top

**The big OEMs and airlines have spent billions on this.** Lufthansa Technik's AVIATAR, GE's predictive analytics on engine health (the LEAP and GEnx engines are streaming sensor data continuously), Boeing's AnalytX, Airbus's Skywise. These platforms ingest live aircraft sensor data, predict component failures, and automatically trigger parts orders. United, Delta, Lufthansa run on this. It's mature.

**Engine OEMs especially are way ahead.** Pratt and GE know roughly when a specific engine on a specific tail number will need a specific part because they're reading the data off it in real time. This is the core of "Power-by-the-Hour" service contracts — they price the contract based on predicted parts consumption.

**Big distributors have decent systems.** AAR, Proponent, Satair all run modern WMS, have demand forecasting models, optimize inventory positioning across global warehouses. Not bleeding-edge AI, but competent supply chain analytics.

**HEICO is data-driven on PMA selection.** They pick which parts to reverse-engineer based on detailed analysis of consumption rates, OEM pricing, market size. That's a quantitative business.

## Where the gap is actually massive

The gap isn't at the top. It's everywhere else, and that's where there's real opportunity.

**The mid-market and below is running on fumes.** Independent brokers, small distributors, regional MROs, charter operator maintenance departments — these run on Excel, email, and ILS searches. Someone calls in needing a part, a person manually searches ILS, calls three suppliers, emails quotes back. The entire workflow is human telephone.

**ILS and PartsBase are 1990s tech.** They're searchable databases. That's it. No predictive layer, no demand intelligence, no automated quoting, no smart matching. Listings are stale, often wrong. Pricing is opaque and negotiated case-by-case.

**Cross-border logistics is genuinely terrible.** Customs documentation, FAA 8130-3 verification, dual-language paperwork, tax structuring — almost entirely manual. A part can sit at GRU customs for two weeks because someone typed an HTS code wrong.

**The long tail of operators has zero predictive maintenance.** A Part 135 operator with eight King Airs has no demand forecasting. Their director of maintenance orders parts when something breaks or when a scheduled inspection is coming up. There's no system saying "based on your fleet composition, utilization, and component life cycles, you'll need these 12 SKUs in the next 90 days."

**Inventory pooling is rare.** Operators hoard parts they rarely need. The same $40K LRU sits on shelves at twelve different operators within 100 miles of each other, used twice a year. No system surfaces this.

## Where AI/data actually creates an edge

A few specific places where it's not vaporware:

**Demand prediction at the long-tail operator level.** If you know an operator's fleet (tail numbers, ages, utilization from ADS-B), the maintenance schedules for those aircraft types, and historical consumption patterns across similar fleets, you can predict what they'll need with surprising accuracy. This is a model OSA could literally build today because the operator entity resolution + ADS-B utilization data already exists in your stack. Nobody is doing this for the mid-market.

**Smart matching and auto-quoting.** RFQ comes in for a part, model parses the part number, cross-references global inventory in real-time across consignment partners, generates a quote in 30 seconds instead of 4 hours. This is a solved technical problem that nobody has shipped well in aviation parts.

**Cross-border trade compliance automation.** HTS classification, FAA 8130-3 validation, ANAC documentation, customs forms, tax regime selection — all of this is structured data work that LLMs handle well. A workflow that turns a parts order into a complete compliant export shipment automatically would be genuinely valuable. This is also where Brazil is uniquely painful and where tooling would compound.

**Inventory pooling network.** A platform where operators consign their excess parts and other operators in the network can access them, with automated trust/condition verification and revenue split. The technical execution is straightforward. The hard part is the cold start on the network — but with OSA's existing operator relationships, you'd start with a built-in seed network.

**ADS-B-based fleet utilization → parts demand signal.** Nobody else has clean ADS-B-to-operator-to-aircraft entity resolution at scale. This is genuinely OSA's structural advantage. If you can see every flight every aircraft makes, you can predict component wear in aggregate at a level the OEMs only see for engines they've sold service contracts on.

## Honest assessment for OSA

This is a real opportunity and the structural fit with what you've already built is unusually clean. Three things to be sober about:

**It's a different physical business.** Even the most data-and-AI-enabled parts brokerage still requires AS9120 certification, traceability documentation, repair station relationships, customs brokerage, physical logistics. The software layer is the wedge but it doesn't replace the operational substrate. You'd need a parts ops person with real industry credibility — this isn't a pure software play.

**The data moat is real but not exclusive.** You have the best operator/aircraft entity graph because you built it. But the parts data (ILS listings, consumption history, OEM pricing) is harder to acquire and others have a head start. Your edge is the operator side, not the parts side. The model would be: own demand intelligence, partner or aggregate for supply.

**The wedge that makes most sense given your existing position** is *parts intelligence as a service* before any actual brokerage. Build the prediction model on top of your existing operator/aircraft graph + ADS-B data + public maintenance data. Show operators "here's what your fleet will need in 90 days." That builds demand-side relationships and proves the data signal is real. Then, only then, add the brokerage execution layer underneath. You're monetizing intelligence first, taking transaction fees second.

This is structurally the same play as OSA Connect on the charter side — own the buyer, surface intelligence, capture the transaction. The parts variant has higher transaction frequency, smaller average ticket, but compounds on operator relationships in a way that strengthens the rest of the OSA stack.

The Brazil angle remains the cleanest beachhead because the cross-border execution complexity is itself a moat — anyone can build a US-only parts intelligence tool, but the US-Brazil corridor with full trade compliance + Portuguese-native operations + real operator relationships is a much narrower competitive field.

## The documents that define parts demand

**Type Certificate Data Sheet (TCDS)** — FAA/ANAC document defining the aircraft's certification basis. Tells you approved engines, props, configurations. Not directly about parts but it bounds what's installed.

**Illustrated Parts Catalog (IPC)** — *this is the actual parts universe*. Every part on the aircraft, by ATA chapter, with part numbers, alternates, applicability by serial number. The IPC for an E175 has tens of thousands of line items. This is the single most important document for parts mapping. OEM-controlled, expensive to access, updated continuously.

**Aircraft Maintenance Manual (AMM)** — tells you *how* to maintain each system, including removal/installation procedures and which parts are consumed in each task. Cross-referenced to the IPC.

**Maintenance Planning Document (MPD)** — the maintenance program. Lists every scheduled task, interval (calendar time, flight hours, cycles), and what parts are consumed. This is where predictive demand actually comes from. An E175 MPD has thousands of tasks at intervals from 100 hours to 12 years.

**Component Maintenance Manual (CMM)** — for each rotable component (LRU), the CMM tells you what piece-parts go inside it during overhaul. Repair stations use these.

**Service Bulletins (SBs) and Airworthiness Directives (ADs)** — modifications and mandatory fixes. When an AD drops, every affected aircraft globally needs the specified parts within a deadline. This is where massive demand spikes come from.

**MEL (Minimum Equipment List)** — list of equipment that *can be inoperative* for a defined period while the aircraft continues to operate. Tells you deferral tolerance, not parts demand. But it tells you which deferred items will *eventually* need a part.

**NEF (Nonessential Equipment and Furnishings)** — items the operator is allowed to defer indefinitely. Lower priority parts demand.

**CDL (Configuration Deviation List)** — what panels/fairings can be missing and the aircraft can still fly. Source of demand for cosmetic and non-critical structural parts.

## The hierarchy of what drives parts demand

There are essentially four demand streams, and they require different data:

**1. Scheduled maintenance demand** — predictable from the MPD + utilization. If you know the MPD task list and the aircraft's flight hours and cycles, you can forecast 80%+ of consumable demand 6-12 months out. This is the highest-confidence prediction layer.

**2. Life-limited parts (LLP) replacement** — parts with a hard cycle/hour limit. Engine LLPs, landing gear, certain structural components. Predictable from utilization and install dates. You can know years in advance when a $200K HPT disk needs replacement.

**3. Unscheduled removal (failure-driven)** — components fail, get removed, get replaced. This is statistical — driven by Mean Time Between Unscheduled Removal (MTBUR) for each component. The data exists at OEM level (they collect warranty and service data) and at large operator level. For small operators, it's anecdotal.

**4. Modification-driven** — ADs, SBs, customer-elected upgrades, avionics retrofits. Episodic but large dollar value.

## What you actually need to build the prediction model

To build a real demand prediction system for a fleet, you need:

- **Tail-number-level fleet data** — make, model, serial, engine serials, install dates, mod status. Public from FAA registry / RAB.
- **Utilization data** — flight hours and cycles per tail. ADS-B gives you a usable proxy at the flight level which you can integrate to hours/cycles.
- **MPD applicability** — which scheduled tasks apply to each aircraft variant. OEM-sourced, sometimes available through MRO partnerships.
- **IPC linkage** — task → required parts. OEM-sourced.
- **Component install date / time-since-new / time-since-overhaul** — this is the hardest data layer. Not public. Lives in operator maintenance tracking systems (CAMP, Flightdocs, Trax, AMOS).
- **AD/SB compliance status** — what's been done, what's pending, by tail.
- **Component reliability statistics (MTBUR)** — for failure-driven prediction. Closely-held by OEMs and large MROs.

## The honest gap analysis

What's freely available:
- FAA registry and TCDS data
- AD and SB lists
- ADS-B utilization
- General industry MTBUR data for common components

What's gated:
- IPC, AMM, MPD, CMM (OEM proprietary, expensive licenses)
- Tail-specific maintenance tracking (operator-owned)
- Component-level install dates and time-in-service (operator-owned)
- True OEM reliability data

What you can derive without OEM access:
- Aircraft type-level scheduled maintenance demand at aggregate level (you can know that 200 PT6 engines in Brazil will collectively need X engine accessories per month even without tail-specific data)
- AD-driven demand spikes (public data, deterministic)
- Utilization-driven life-limit forecasts at aggregate level
- Geographic concentration of demand

## Where this lands for OSA

The realistic v0 prediction model wouldn't try to be tail-specific — that requires data you can't get without operator partnerships. It would be **fleet-aggregate demand prediction**: given the population of aircraft of type X in region Y with average utilization Z, predict total parts consumption by SKU category. That's enough to be useful for distributors deciding what to stock, for brokers deciding what to source, and for operators benchmarking their own consumption.

The full tail-specific model becomes possible later, but only via partnerships with maintenance tracking platforms (CAMP, Flightdocs) or direct integration with operator MRO systems. That's a Phase 2 conversation that requires having earned trust through Phase 1.

Interesting future moat but not buildable today.

The full picture requires layering certification data, scheduled maintenance, life limits, ADs/SBs, and reliability statistics on top of fleet and utilization data.

## Claude

Quick refresh on both:

## IPC — Illustrated Parts Catalog

The OEM-published document that lists every replaceable part on a specific aircraft, organized by ATA chapter, with part numbers, illustrations, and vendor data. It's the *physical assembly tree* — you can traverse from the aircraft down to subsystems down to individual components. For a King Air 350, the IPC tells you every part on the aircraft and where it goes.

What the IPC does:
- Identifies parts by part number
- Shows assembly relationships (where each part fits)
- Lists vendor/supplier data
- Indicates effectivity by serial number (which parts apply to which build of the aircraft)

What the IPC does *not* do:
- Tell you when parts get consumed (that's the MPD — Maintenance Planning Document)
- Tell you how to remove/install (that's the AMM — Aircraft Maintenance Manual)
- Tell you reliability or expected life (that's OEM-held data)

Access tiers we discussed: free for piston (Continental, Lycoming portals), subscription for turbine GA and business jets ($500–5K/year typically), expensive for commercial transport (tens of thousands per fleet).

## Gate lists — the deferral framework

You probably mean the three documents that define what equipment can be inoperative on an aircraft:

**MEL — Minimum Equipment List**
The list of equipment that *can be inoperative* for a defined period while the aircraft continues to operate legally. Each MEL item has a category (A, B, C, D) defining how long deferral is allowed before the part must be repaired or replaced. The MEL is operator-specific, derived from the OEM's MMEL (Master MEL) and approved by the regulator (FAA, ANAC, etc).

Example: "Inoperative #2 fuel quantity indicator — Category C, 10 days to repair."

**NEF — Nonessential Equipment and Furnishings**
Items the operator is allowed to defer *indefinitely* because they don't affect airworthiness. Cabin reading lights, broken seatback trays, cosmetic items. Lower priority parts demand because there's no deadline forcing a fix.

**CDL — Configuration Deviation List**
What external panels, fairings, or aerodynamic items can be missing while the aircraft still flies. Different from MEL because it's about *physical missing parts*, not inoperative equipment. Often comes with performance penalties (reduced max weight, lower altitude, etc).

Example: "Missing right wing-to-fuselage fairing #3 — flight allowed with 200 lb payload reduction until repair."

## Why these matter together

The reason I brought these up earlier: the IPC defines the *universe of parts*. The MEL/NEF/CDL define which parts an operator is currently *running deferred on*. If you knew an operator's open MEL items in real time, you'd know exactly which parts they need but haven't ordered yet. That's a powerful AOG demand signal — but it lives in dispatch systems, not public data.

For demand prediction, the more accessible data is:
- IPC (parts universe) — accessible at varying cost
- MPD (scheduled maintenance intervals) — OEM-controlled
- AD/SB lists (mandatory and recommended actions) — public from FAA/ANAC
- Utilization data — derivable from ADS-B
- Fleet composition — public from FAA registry

Open MEL items would be the cherry on top, but require operator data partnerships to access.

## Claude

ATA stands for **Air Transport Association** (now called Airlines for America, A4A — but everyone still says "ATA" because the standards came from when it was called that).

## What ATA actually means in this context

When people say "ATA" in aviation parts and maintenance, they almost always mean **ATA chapters** or **ATA Spec 100** — a standardized numbering system for aircraft systems.

Every aircraft, regardless of manufacturer, organizes its technical documentation by the same chapter numbers. This is why an A&P mechanic can pick up an IPC for a Boeing 737, an Airbus A320, an Embraer E175, or a King Air, and find the hydraulics section in the same chapter every time.

## The chapter structure

The major chapters everyone uses:

- **ATA 21** — Air Conditioning
- **ATA 22** — Auto Flight (autopilot)
- **ATA 23** — Communications
- **ATA 24** — Electrical Power
- **ATA 25** — Equipment/Furnishings
- **ATA 26** — Fire Protection
- **ATA 27** — Flight Controls
- **ATA 28** — Fuel
- **ATA 29** — Hydraulic Power
- **ATA 30** — Ice and Rain Protection
- **ATA 31** — Indicating/Recording Systems
- **ATA 32** — Landing Gear
- **ATA 33** — Lights
- **ATA 34** — Navigation
- **ATA 35** — Oxygen
- **ATA 36** — Pneumatic
- **ATA 49** — Airborne Auxiliary Power (APU)
- **ATA 52** — Doors
- **ATA 53** — Fuselage
- **ATA 54** — Nacelles/Pylons
- **ATA 55** — Stabilizers
- **ATA 56** — Windows
- **ATA 57** — Wings
- **ATA 71-80** — Powerplant (engines)

There are sub-chapters too. ATA 32-40 is wheels and brakes specifically (within Landing Gear). ATA 27-50 is leading edge slats specifically (within Flight Controls).

## Why this matters for parts intelligence

The ATA chapter system is the *organizing principle* of the entire industry. Specifically:

**IPC organization** — every Illustrated Parts Catalog is structured by ATA chapter. You don't search by part name, you traverse "ATA 32 Landing Gear → 32-40 Wheels and Brakes → wheel half assembly → part number."

**Distributor organization** — when distributors describe their inventory, they use ATA chapters. "We're strong in ATA 32, ATA 49, and ATA 79." This means landing gear, APU, and engine oil systems.

**Pricing and reliability data** — when industry data references "ATA 71-80 represents 30% of aftermarket spend," they're saying engine parts are 30% of aftermarket revenue.

**HEICO's PMA portfolio** — described as "covers nearly every ATA chapter" — meaning they make replacement parts across the whole aircraft.

**Mechanic workflow** — A&Ps think in ATA chapters. "I'm troubleshooting a 21 issue" means an air conditioning problem.

**Maintenance reserves** — appraisers structure maintenance cash flow models by ATA chapter to forecast major events.

## The deeper specs

Beyond chapters, ATA publishes detailed standards:

**ATA Spec 100** — the original technical documentation standard, defines how to write maintenance manuals. Older but still referenced for legacy aircraft.

**ATA Spec 2200 (iSpec 2200)** — modern XML-based version, defines how technical documentation is structured digitally. This is what enables EPC (Electronic Parts Catalog) software to work across manufacturers.

**ATA Spec 2000** — defines how parts ordering and supply chain data is exchanged electronically. Most B2B parts systems run on Spec 2000 messaging. ILS, distributor systems, OEM portals all speak Spec 2000.

**ATA Spec 2300** — flight operations data exchange.

## For your purposes

When you see "ATA chapter" in any of the parts conversations, just translate it to "the aircraft system it belongs to." It's the universal taxonomy that makes parts intelligence possible across manufacturers.

If you were building OSA's parts intelligence layer, ATA chapter would be one of your core data primitives — every part, every demand signal, every distributor relationship organizes by it. The fact that the industry already has this standard in place is genuinely useful — you don't have to invent the taxonomy, you just adopt it.

How they make it hard:

**Proprietary parts.** Many critical components are designed so only the OEM can supply them. They hold the engineering drawings. No PMA shop can reverse-engineer without significant cost.

**Pricing power on captive parts.** A widget that costs $50 to make sells for $2,000 from the OEM because no one else can make it. The customer has no alternative.

**Service contracts that lock customers in.** Power-by-the-Hour, FlightHour, EngineWise — the OEM bundles parts + maintenance + warranty into a per-hour fee. Once an operator is on PBH, they can't easily buy parts from anyone else.

**Software locks.** Modern engines and avionics have authentication that makes third-party parts not function or trigger maintenance flags. Aerospace is becoming more like John Deere — your tractor only runs on their authorized parts.

**Warranty implications.** Operators are told (sometimes accurately, sometimes not) that using non-OEM parts voids warranty. Even when PMA parts are FAA-approved equivalents.

**Data restrictions.** OEMs do not share component reliability data with PMA developers, distributors, or appraisers. The data that would help anyone else build a competitive offering stays inside.

**Litigation.** OEMs have sued PMA developers, sued PMA-friendly publications, sued operators who use too many PMA parts on their fleets.

This is why HEICO is so structurally interesting. They've spent 65 years winning this fight — getting 19,000 FAA approvals, fighting OEM legal pushback, building airline relationships willing to specify HEICO parts. That moat is genuinely deep and took decades to build.

## The data layers you need

There are seven distinct data layers, and you need most of them to do real prediction. Missing one or two is workable, missing four is fatal.

**Layer 1 — Aircraft identity and configuration**
What aircraft exist, what they are, how they're built.
- Tail number / registration
- Make, model, variant
- Serial number (MSN)
- Engine model and serial numbers
- Avionics suite
- Modifications and STCs installed
- Year of manufacture

Sources: FAA registry (free, downloadable monthly), ANAC RAB for Brazil (public), TCDS data (public), operator self-reporting for mods.

Difficulty: Easy. This is the layer you already have largely built.

**Layer 2 — Parts universe (the IPC tree)**
Every replaceable part on each aircraft type, organized by ATA chapter, with effectivity by serial number.
- Part numbers
- Assembly relationships (parent-child)
- Vendor data
- Effectivity ranges (which serial numbers each part applies to)
- Supersession history (newer part numbers replacing older)

Sources: OEM IPC subscriptions, OEM portals, sometimes leaked PDFs floating around (not a basis for production use).

Difficulty: Variable. Free for piston, $500-5K/year per platform for turbine GA, expensive for commercial transport.

**Layer 3 — Maintenance program (the MPD)**
The schedule of when things need to happen.
- Task list with intervals (calendar time, flight hours, cycles)
- Parts consumed per task
- Threshold and repeat intervals
- Special conditions (corrosion zones, high-utilization adjustments)
- Cross-reference to IPC part numbers

Sources: OEM Maintenance Planning Documents, sometimes available through MRO partnerships, operator's own maintenance tracking.

Difficulty: Hard to access standalone. Easier through CMMS partnerships or operator data sharing.

**Layer 4 — Life-limited parts (LLP) tracking**
Parts with hard cycle/hour limits that must be replaced regardless of condition.
- LLP catalog by engine model
- Cycle limits per part
- Current time-since-new for each LLP
- Disk tables for engines

Sources: OEM-published LLP catalogs (limited), engine maintenance tracking systems, operator records.

Difficulty: Moderate. Some data is public, tail-specific install dates require operator data.

**Layer 5 — Compliance status (ADs and SBs)**
Mandatory and recommended modifications.
- AD list by aircraft type (FAA, ANAC, EASA, all regulators)
- SB list by aircraft type (OEM-issued)
- Compliance deadlines
- Parts required for compliance
- Per-tail compliance status

Sources: FAA AD database (free), ANAC IS database (free), OEM SB portals (subscription), per-tail status from operator records.

Difficulty: Public data is easy. Per-tail compliance status requires operator data.

**Layer 6 — Utilization data**
How much each aircraft is actually flying.
- Flight hours per period
- Flight cycles per period
- Geographic profile (corrosion exposure, altitude profile)
- Mission type (training, charter, ag, scheduled)

Sources: ADS-B observation (free, you already have this), operator self-reporting, maintenance tracking systems.

Difficulty: Easy at aggregate level from ADS-B, harder for tail-specific cycle counts which require flight log data.

**Layer 7 — Reliability statistics**
How often things actually fail in service.
- Mean Time Between Unscheduled Removal (MTBUR) by component
- Failure mode distributions
- Environmental and utilization adjustments
- Fleet-wide reliability data

Sources: OEM warranty data (held tightly), large-MRO databases (proprietary), industry reports (limited), operator self-reporting (fragmented), Veryon/CAMP data (their customers only).

Difficulty: This is the hardest layer to acquire. The good data is held by OEMs and not shared. Public data exists but is sparse.

## What you can actually build with each combination

Different combinations of these layers produce different fidelity of prediction:

**Layers 1 + 5 + 6 = Compliance demand prediction**
Without IPC or MPD, you can still predict AD/SB-driven demand because that data is public. "There are 47 King Air 350s in Brazil; new AD requires X part by deadline; here's the demand spike."

**Layers 1 + 2 + 3 + 6 = Scheduled maintenance prediction**
With aircraft fleet, IPC tree, MPD intervals, and utilization, you can predict scheduled task demand at fleet level. "These 47 aircraft, given current utilization, will collectively consume X of part number Y in next 90 days." Doesn't tell you which specific tail will need the part when.

**Layers 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = Tail-specific predictable demand**
Adding LLP tracking and per-tail AD compliance status gets you to "this specific aircraft will need this specific part by this specific date" for the predictable categories — scheduled tasks, life-limited replacements, AD compliance.

**All seven layers = Full predictive demand including failure-driven**
Adding reliability statistics gets you the unscheduled removal layer. Now you can predict not just what's scheduled, but what's *likely* to fail. This is what GE and Rolls-Royce do for their engines, and what Veryon does at the airline level.

## What exists today

**LiveATC.net** is the dominant platform. Volunteer-operated network of receivers feeding live ATC audio from towers, approach, center facilities globally. Free streaming, archive available. Most aviation enthusiasts and pilots know it. Good US and major-airport coverage; spotty in Brazil and emerging markets.

What none of these do well is the part you're hinting at: **structured, searchable, machine-readable correlation between ATC voice events and specific aircraft tracks.**

## The technical possibility

Raw ATC audio is just audio. To make it useful as data, you need three steps:

**Step 1 — Speech-to-text on ATC audio.** This used to be terrible because ATC audio has heavy accents, fast speech, callsigns that aren't natural language, frequency noise, and procedural shorthand. Modern speech models (Whisper, especially fine-tuned on aviation corpora) have gotten dramatically better. There are open ATC corpora — ATCO2, LDC ATCC — that researchers use for fine-tuning.

**Step 2 — Entity extraction from transcribed ATC.** Pull callsigns ("United 247 climb and maintain flight level 350"), runways, headings, frequencies, instructions, clearances. Standard NER on ATC-fine-tuned models. Doable.

**Step 3 — Correlate ATC events with ADS-B tracks.** Match callsign to tail number (callsign-to-tail mapping is mostly observable from ADS-B Mode S extended squitter data). Time-align voice events with track events. Now you have "at 14:23:11, KORD Tower cleared UAL247 for takeoff runway 10L, and at 14:23:48, the aircraft began takeoff roll based on ADS-B."

Each step is technically achievable today. People are doing all three in academic settings. The infrastructure to do it at production scale across many facilities is the work.

## Where this gets useful

For OSA's intelligence stack specifically, ATC correlation would unlock several things ADS-B alone can't see:

**Pre-departure intelligence.** ADS-B doesn't show an aircraft until it's powered and squawking, often after taxi out. ATC ground frequency reveals intent earlier — clearance delivery, taxi instructions. For charter and operator intelligence, knowing an aircraft is *about to* depart 20-30 minutes before ADS-B sees it is operationally meaningful.

**IFR clearance routing.** ATC clearance audio reveals the actual filed and amended route, including reroutes due to weather or traffic. ADS-B shows what happened; ATC shows what was planned. The delta is itself information about delays, weather impact, traffic flow management.

**AOG and divert events.** "Mayday," "declaring an emergency," "requesting priority handling," "diverting to nearest" — these phrases on ATC audio are direct signals of high-value events for an aviation intelligence platform. AOG events drive parts demand spikes. Diverts disrupt flight schedules. Real-time detection of these events from ATC audio is faster than any other source.

**Ground operations intelligence.** Tower frequencies reveal taxi times, runway congestion, ground stop intelligence. Ramp frequencies reveal handling operations. None of this is in ADS-B.

**Brazilian intelligence specifically.** Brazilian ATC (DECEA) operates somewhat differently from FAA — Portuguese language for domestic operations mixed with English for international, different procedural standards. ## The legal and ethical territory

This is where it gets careful. A few things to know:

**Listening to ATC audio is legal in the US.** ATC frequencies are public radio communications. LiveATC has operated for 20+ years without major legal challenge. The FCC permits monitoring; broadcast restrictions in the Communications Act apply to certain *content categories* (cellular, encrypted, military) but not to ATC.

**International varies.** Some countries technically prohibit recording or rebroadcasting public radio communications even when the listening itself is legal. Brazil's regulations are murky. The UK has restrictions. France is restrictive. Operating in some jurisdictions requires understanding local rules.

**Aircraft callsign-to-operator mapping has privacy implications.** ATC reveals tail numbers and operator identities. For business aviation specifically, where many owners use blocking programs (LADD, FAA PIA), correlating ATC with ADS-B essentially defeats those privacy mechanisms. This is the same reason ADS-B Exchange got controversial. Doing it at scale and commercializing it would attract attention from operators who specifically pay for privacy.

**Real-time ATC re-broadcasting has FCC and aviation industry sensitivities.** Even if technically legal, building a product that surfaces ATC audio publicly would put you in tension with controllers, operators, and the broader industry. The smart approach is *not* to rebroadcast — to use the audio as input data for derived intelligence products and discard the audio after extraction.

## The two tiers of SWIM access

**Tier 1 — SCDS (SWIM Cloud Distribution Service) via SWIFT Portal**

All product(s) provided by SCDS have been pre-approved for public release by the National Data Release Board, intended for non-NAS, non-Operational use.

How it works:

- Sign the SAA electronically
- Get JMS connection credentials
- Connect your Solace JMS consumer
- Receive data

What you get on this tier: the publicly-released SWIM services — flight data (SFDPS), traffic flow data (TFMS publish-only), aeronautical information, weather data, NAS Common Reference (NCR), ITWS weather. Most of what we discussed wanting.

This is the harder path for the gated data. To access certain services not on SWIFT Portal (CDM Data Elements, full operational TFMS request/reply, certain restricted feeds), you submit a request to Data-To-Industry@faa.gov and the request is reviewed in accordance with FAA policy.

Beyond that, NESG connections require physical or networked colocation, FTI (FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure) integration, formal interconnection and data sharing agreements, business rules. ## What MLAT is

MLAT (Multilateration) is how you locate aircraft that don't broadcast position data — typically older aircraft with Mode S transponders but no ADS-B Out, or aircraft transmitting only Mode A/C transponder signals.

The technique: when an aircraft transmits any signal (transponder reply, Mode S downlink), multiple ground receivers detect that same signal at slightly different times based on their distance from the aircraft. By measuring the Time Difference of Arrival (TDOA) across at least four receivers with synchronized clocks, you can triangulate the aircraft's position geometrically.

Three or four receivers in different locations + nanosecond-precision time sync = aircraft position, even without ADS-B Out.

## Why MLAT matters specifically

Here's where this gets relevant to what we've been discussing:

**Older aircraft don't have ADS-B Out.** Despite the FAA's 2020 ADS-B Out mandate for most controlled airspace, exemptions and non-compliant aircraft still exist. Many older business jets, helicopters, ag aircraft, experimental, and aircraft operating outside ADS-B-required airspace fly without ADS-B Out. They're invisible to pure ADS-B.

**LADD/PIA aircraft sometimes try to mask via callsign rotation.** ADS-B-Out broadcasts can be filtered by aircraft, but transponder Mode S downlinks contain the ICAO 24-bit address that's harder to obscure. MLAT picks up these aircraft regardless.

**International operations differ.** Brazil's ADS-B Out compliance is uneven. ANAC mandate enforcement varies by aircraft category. Many Brazilian Part 135, ag aviation, and helicopter operators run aircraft without ADS-B Out. For Brazilian operator intelligence, MLAT coverage matters more than US.

**Ground tracking.** Aircraft on the ground often don't transmit position via ADS-B but do respond to transponder interrogations. MLAT gives you ground movement data that pure ADS-B misses.

So MLAT isn't a backup to ADS-B — it's a complement that captures a meaningful slice of traffic ADS-B can't see.

## Who provides MLAT today

The landscape:

**Flightradar24** — historically had the strongest MLAT capability among consumer aggregators because of their dense European receiver network. They invested heavily in MLAT infrastructure for years. Their global MLAT coverage is genuinely good in metropolitan areas.

**Coverage is geographically uneven.** Major metropolitan areas with lots of enthusiast feeders get good MLAT — NYC, LA, London, Frankfurt, Tokyo. Rural areas, ocean overflights, remote regions get poor or no MLAT.

**Brazil specifically has weak MLAT coverage.** Brazilian feeder density is much lower than US/Europe. The same aircraft you'd track easily over the US might be invisible over interior Brazil unless you've built your own receiver network there.

**MLAT accuracy is meaningfully worse than ADS-B.** ADS-B reports GPS-derived position to within meters. MLAT-derived position is typically accurate to a few hundred meters to a couple kilometers depending on receiver geometry. Good enough for tracking, not good enough for precise approach analysis.

**Update rates are slower.** MLAT positions update at the rate of transponder transmissions (every few seconds typically), often less frequently than ADS-B's 1-2 second cadence.

## The technology stack from ground to orbit

There are five distinct surveillance technologies in production use today, each filling specific gaps. Here's how they actually work and what they're good for.

**1. Terrestrial ADS-B (the foundation)**

Aircraft broadcasts position via 1090 MHz (commercial) or 978 MHz UAT (US GA below 18,000 ft). Ground receivers within ~250 nm line-of-sight pick it up. This is the bulk of what you've been thinking about — community feeds, your own SDRs, the Beachhead Network.

Strengths: free, low-latency (1-2 second updates), ubiquitous in populated areas
Weaknesses: line-of-sight only, no oceanic, gaps over remote regions, fails over mountains

**2. MLAT (we just covered)**

Triangulates position from 3-4+ ground receivers via TDOA. Picks up aircraft without ADS-B Out via Mode S transponder replies.

Strengths: catches non-ADS-B aircraft, ground tracking, complements ADS-B
Weaknesses: requires receiver density, lower accuracy, geographically dependent

**3. Satellite ADS-B (Aireon)**

This is the headline one. Aireon's ADS-B receivers are hosted payloads on Iridium NEXT — 66 LEO satellites in orbit. Each Iridium NEXT satellite is able to communicate with its neighboring satellites through "crosslinks." Since the network resides in space, ground stations aren't needed.

The flow: aircraft sends ADS-B message to satellite payload, payload transfers via Iridium satellite network back to earth, then to Aireon's Teleport Network, then to Hosted Payload Operations Center, then to Aireon Data Processing, then to customer. Entire process takes less than 2 seconds.

Powered by Iridium's networked constellation of 66 satellites, Aireon ADS-B provides continuous air traffic surveillance to areas of the world that previously had no access to this information, including over oceans, polar regions, mountainous regions, jungles, deserts, and conflicted airspace. As the world's only global, space-based ADS-B surveillance provider, Aireon offers 100% worldwide coverage across all types of terrain, including oceanic and remote regions.

Status today: production. Multiple ANSPs have adopted it as primary surveillance — NAV CANADA, AirNav Ireland, ENAV, NATS, Naviair, and most recently the Civil Aviation Authority of Malaysia signed an agreement for EASA-certified space-based ADS-B data covering the oceanic service volume of the Kuala Lumpur FIR West Region.

Aireon has also added new capabilities recently: GNSS Interference Detection and Position Assurance — by leveraging space-based ADS-B, Aireon can identify GNSS anomalies and independently validate an aircraft's position, even during GPS jamming or spoofing events. Aireon now derives turbulence information using real-time ADS-B vertical rate data from more than 15,000 aircraft in flight at any given moment.

Distribution: Aireon sells primarily to ANSPs and major aviation customers. Recently Flightradar24 expanded global coverage with Aireon space-based ADS-B data, with Aireon positions displayed in 15-minute increments when the aircraft is outside terrestrial coverage areas. The partnership is reciprocal — Aireon's commercial customers now have access to Flightradar24 data. FlightAware also resells Aireon — the first and only 100% global flight tracking solution for airlines and business aviation, supporting seamless integration through streaming data APIs.

**4. Spire Aviation (the cubesat alternative)**

Spire operates a constellation of CubeSats with ADS-B receivers. Smaller satellites, "bent-pipe" architecture (no inter-satellite crosslinks — they downlink to ground stations).

Strengths: lower price than Aireon, commercial API access, global coverage
Weaknesses: Spire satellites are CubeSat class with limited power, attitude control and basic radio frequency hardware not capable of meeting ATS surveillance level availability requirements. CubeSat bent pipe LEO constellations require a substantial amount of ground stations. Update rates and coverage gaps that don't matter for tracking but disqualify it for ATC use.

For commercial users (not ATC), Spire's data is genuinely useful. They're a real Aireon alternative for tracking (not safety-of-life surveillance).

**5. ADS-C and HF/satcom datalink (legacy oceanic)**

The old way oceanic flights got tracked. ADS-C was designed as an oceanic communication tool. ADS-C allows controllers to monitor aircraft progress, not tactically manage. ICAO's 15-minute tracking standard. Operational since 1990. Inmarsat and Iridium operate the underlying datalinks. Being supplanted by space-based ADS-B but still in use.

## Other surveillance technologies worth knowing

**6. Wide-Area Multilateration (WAM)**

Like MLAT but operated as formal infrastructure by ANSPs. Used in mountainous regions where radar coverage is poor (parts of Austria, Norway, Alaska, Australia). Higher accuracy than informal community MLAT because of dedicated receivers and engineered geometry.

**7. Primary Surveillance Radar (PSR)**

The original. Sends out radar pulse, measures reflection. Doesn't require aircraft cooperation — sees stealth, no transponder, hostile aircraft. Still operated by FAA and military. Not generally accessible to commercial users.

**8. Secondary Surveillance Radar (SSR) / Mode S**

Interrogates aircraft transponders, gets identity and altitude back. The basis of legacy ATC surveillance. Being supplanted by ADS-B but still operational. Not accessible to commercial users directly.

**9. Cooperative satellite-based surveillance (emerging)**

Beyond Aireon and Spire. Multiple constellations being proposed or launched. Iceye and others doing radar imaging satellites that can incidentally observe aircraft. Mostly experimental for aviation.

**10. Aviation cellular (planned)**

5G-based aviation networks for low-altitude UAS surveillance. Not yet relevant for traditional aviation but emerging for drone integration.

**11. ATC voice (we discussed)**

Indirect but valuable — ATC reveals aircraft positions through clearances and instructions even when surveillance fails.

**12. Operational data (flight plans, dispatch, ACARS)**

Not surveillance per se but orthogonal data that fills temporal gaps. SWIM provides flight plans, ACARS provides operational position reports, dispatch software provides intent.

SAR stands for **Synthetic Aperture Radar**. It's a type of imaging satellite that uses radar instead of cameras to "see" the earth, and it has properties that make it genuinely different from regular satellite imagery.

## How SAR actually works

A normal optical satellite is basically a really good camera in space. It collects sunlight reflected off the earth and produces images you'd recognize as photos.

A SAR satellite is fundamentally different. It actively transmits microwave radar pulses down at the earth, and measures what bounces back. The satellite is its own light source. By analyzing the timing, phase, and intensity of returned signals as the satellite moves through orbit, it constructs a high-resolution image.

The "synthetic aperture" part is the clever bit. To get high resolution from radar, you'd normally need a very large antenna. SAR fakes a large antenna by combining multiple radar measurements taken as the satellite moves along its orbit. The motion creates a "synthetic" aperture much larger than the physical antenna, which is what gives modern SAR satellites resolution down to 16-50 cm.

## What this means in practice

The properties that emerge from this approach are what make SAR interesting:

**Sees through clouds.** Microwave radar passes through cloud cover, smoke, haze, and most weather. Optical satellites are useless when the target is cloudy. SAR works regardless of weather.

**Works at night.** No sunlight required since the satellite provides its own illumination. Optical satellites are useless on the night side of earth. SAR works 24/7.

**Detects metal especially well.** Aircraft, ships, vehicles, and infrastructure show up clearly because metal reflects radar strongly. Wood and water absorb more, vegetation has characteristic patterns. This makes SAR particularly good at detecting man-made objects.

**Can detect ground deformation.** Repeated SAR observations over the same area can detect millimeter-level changes — useful for monitoring infrastructure, geological events, even slight building settlement.

**Penetrates surfaces partially.** Depending on radar frequency, SAR can see through dry sand, light vegetation, and shallow water. Some uses include archaeological surveys and underground infrastructure mapping.

**Limitations.** SAR images don't look like photos — they're grayscale and require interpretation. Resolution at the high end is good (16-50cm) but not as fine as the best optical (30cm). Processing is computationally expensive. Tasking is more complex than optical.

## Who operates SAR satellites today

The commercial SAR landscape has gotten interesting in the last few years:

**Capella Space** — US-based, ~10 satellites, claims 50cm resolution. Sells imagery via API. Strong commercial focus, decent revisit rates.

**ICEYE** — Finnish company, ~30+ satellites. The largest commercial SAR constellation. Originally focused on maritime monitoring but expanded broadly. Strong revisit rates.

**Umbra** — US-based, fewer satellites but very high resolution (claims 16cm). Premium pricing.

**Synspective** — Japanese, smaller constellation, growing.

**Airbus** — operates TerraSAR-X and PAZ satellites, established commercial SAR provider, premium pricing.

**Maxar** — operating optical mostly but also SAR through partnerships and the WorldView Legion constellation.

**Government/military operators** — extensive but inaccessible. NRO, ESA, JAXA, ISRO all operate SAR satellites for intelligence and earth observation. Not commercial.

Pricing: tactical SAR imagery typically $300-2,000 per scene depending on resolution, urgency, and provider. A "scene" covers something like 5km x 5km. For aviation use cases, that's enough to image one airport per scene.

## SAR for aviation specifically

Where SAR is genuinely useful for aviation intelligence:

**Aircraft census at airports.** Buy SAR images of major airports periodically, run computer vision to identify aircraft types and count them. You get fleet inventory data regardless of weather, day or night. Useful for tracking what's parked at MRO facilities, lessor warehouses, manufacturer factories, military bases.

**MRO facility monitoring.** A SAR image of a major MRO facility tells you how many aircraft are in the hangar queue, how the work-in-progress is changing over time. Combined with public flight data, you can build pretty accurate models of MRO activity that nobody else has.

**Lessor parking facilities.** Aircraft on lease return get parked at desert storage facilities (Roswell, Mojave, Victorville, Pinal Park). SAR can census these facilities and track aircraft that move in and out. Lessors and ABS investors care about this for collateral monitoring.

**Manufacturer inventory.** What's parked at Embraer in São José dos Campos? At Boeing Renton? At Airbus Toulouse? SAR can answer this regardless of weather or time of day. Combined with serial number tracking, you'd have factory throughput intelligence.

**Disaster response.** When a hurricane or earthquake disrupts an airport, SAR can image immediately even if weather makes optical impossible. Useful for insurance, response coordination, business continuity.

**Surface surveillance at airports.** ASDE-X data is gated. SAR can give you alternative ground operations visibility at major airports, useful for understanding congestion, taxi times, gate utilization.

**Non-cooperative aircraft detection.** This is the big one for the question you asked earlier. SAR can detect aircraft on the ground regardless of whether they have transponders. So military aircraft, aircraft with transponders off, aircraft at remote strips — SAR sees them. For tracking actual aviation activity in remote regions where cooperative tracking fails, SAR is one of the few options.

## What SAR doesn't do well for aviation

To be honest about limitations:

**Real-time tracking of flying aircraft.** SAR is fundamentally a snapshot technology. By the time you've tasked, captured, downlinked, and processed a SAR image, the aircraft is long gone. You don't track flights with SAR — you take occasional pictures.

**Continuous monitoring.** Even with constellation revisit rates of multiple times per day, you're looking at hourly snapshots, not real-time. Aircraft moving at airline speed cover hundreds of miles between revisits.

**Cost at scale.** Continuous SAR monitoring of large areas adds up fast. $1,000 per scene times daily monitoring of 50 airports times 365 days = $18M/year. The economics only work for tactical, targeted, high-value use cases.

**Type identification at low resolution.** SAR can detect that an aircraft is there, often identify it as "small twin" vs "narrow body" vs "wide body." Specific type identification requires the highest resolution and good geometry, which costs more.

## What you're actually building, stripped to first principles

Forget the aviation-specific framing for a moment. At the deepest level, you're building a **probabilistic state estimation system over a population of physical assets evolving through time**.

That's it. Every aircraft is a physical system in some state. The state changes due to deterministic processes (utilization, scheduled maintenance), stochastic processes (component failures), and exogenous events (regulations, modifications). You observe noisy partial signals about the state. You want to estimate the current state and predict future states well enough to make decisions.

This framing matters because it tells you which sciences and mathematical frameworks are actually relevant rather than which ones sound impressive.

## The core mathematical problems you're actually solving

There are five distinct mathematical problems underneath what we've been discussing, and recognizing them as separate is critical:

**Problem 1: Entity resolution**

The science: probabilistic record linkage and graph theory.

You have multiple noisy data sources naming the same real-world entities differently. FAA registry says one thing, ANAC says another, ADS-B beacon says a third, the operator's website says a fourth. You need to determine when "ABC Aviation Holdings LLC" and "ABC Aviation, LLC" and "ABC Av." are the same legal entity.

The relevant science:
- Fellegi-Sunter probabilistic record linkage (1969) is the foundation
- Splink (your existing approach) implements modern probabilistic linkage with Expectation-Maximization for parameter learning
- Graph neural networks for cross-entity inference (when you've linked some entities, you can infer others through graph structure)
- Active learning for human-in-the-loop confirmation of uncertain matches

What this gets you: a clean substrate where every operator, aircraft, person, and facility is a single canonical entity with all variants linked.

**Problem 2: State estimation under partial observation**

The science: Bayesian filtering, hidden Markov models, Kalman filtering and its nonlinear extensions.

For each aircraft, you observe partial signals — ADS-B utilization, maintenance events when reported, public AD compliance — but the true state (component time-since-overhaul, current health, accumulated wear) is hidden. You need to estimate the hidden state from observable signals.

The relevant science:
- Hidden Markov Models for state transitions through discrete maintenance phases
- Kalman filtering for continuous state estimation (component time-in-service evolving with utilization)
- Particle filtering for nonlinear state evolution (component degradation)
- Variational Bayes methods when exact inference is intractable

This is the mathematical core of what GE and Rolls-Royce do with their digital twins. The same math, applied with less observation richness, produces useful estimates at fleet scale.

**Problem 3: Survival analysis and reliability engineering**

The science: time-to-event statistics, reliability theory.

For each component, you want to estimate "how long until this fails or needs replacement?" The data is censored — you don't observe failures for components still in service. The hazard rate is non-constant — components have infant mortality, useful life, and wear-out phases (the bathtub curve).

The relevant science:
- Weibull survival analysis (the classical approach for component lifetime modeling)
- Cox proportional hazards models when you have covariates (utilization, environment, operator quality)
- Accelerated failure time models for environmental adjustment
- Bayesian survival analysis for combining engineering knowledge (priors) with observed data
- Recurrent event analysis for components that fail-and-replace multiple times

This is what feeds your Mean Time Between Unscheduled Removal predictions. The mathematics is mature; the constraint is data availability.

**Problem 4: Demand forecasting under hierarchical structure**

The science: hierarchical time series forecasting, queuing theory.

Parts demand exists at multiple aggregation levels — total fleet, aircraft type, operator, individual tail. The forecasts at different levels need to be consistent (operator-level forecasts should sum to type-level forecasts). The demand has multiple components — scheduled (predictable from MPD), AD-driven (deterministic from regulation), failure-driven (stochastic from reliability), modification-driven (episodic).

The relevant science:
- Hierarchical forecasting (Hyndman et al.) for reconciling forecasts across levels
- Intermittent demand models (Croston, SBA, TSB) for slow-moving parts
- State-space models for combining trend and seasonality
- Compound Poisson processes for failure-driven demand
- Queuing theory for MRO capacity-constrained demand

What this gets you: predictions like "Brazilian PT6 fleet will need 1,840 ATA-79 oil system parts in next 90 days, with these aircraft being highest-probability sources of demand."

**Problem 5: Causal inference vs correlation**

The science: causal inference, instrumental variables, quasi-experiments.

You'll observe correlations everywhere. Operators with more PMA parts have lower maintenance costs. Aircraft at certain MROs have shorter turnaround times. These could be causal or could be selection effects (PMA-friendly operators are also better-managed; better-run airlines pick more efficient MROs). For OSA's intelligence outputs to be actionable, you need to distinguish.

The relevant science:
- Pearl's structural causal models and do-calculus
- Instrumental variables for natural experiments
- Difference-in-differences for policy changes (AD compliance, regulatory shifts)
- Synthetic control methods for fleet-level interventions
- Propensity score matching for operator comparisons

This is what lets you answer "would switching to this MRO actually reduce your turnaround time, or do faster operators just choose this MRO?"

## The specific scientific tools that compound

Beyond the five problem domains, several specific scientific approaches compound across the entire platform:

**Graph neural networks for the unified entity graph**

Once you have the operator-aircraft-parts-facility-event graph, GNNs let you do inference across the graph structure. Predict the operator quality of a new operator from its graph neighborhood. Identify likely parts failure on aircraft you don't have direct observation of by similarity to aircraft you do. The graph structure itself becomes a feature space.

The relevant science: message-passing neural networks, graph attention networks, heterogeneous graph neural networks for typed entities, graph contrastive learning for unsupervised representations.

**Self-supervised learning from observation streams**

You have massive observation streams (ADS-B, ATC voice, satellite imagery) without labels. Self-supervised methods learn representations from the data structure itself.

For ADS-B trajectories: contrastive learning where similar trajectories cluster, autoencoder approaches for anomaly detection, transformer-based sequence modeling for trajectory prediction.

For ATC voice: pre-trained Whisper-style models fine-tuned on aviation, with self-supervised pre-training on unlabeled aviation audio extending the corpus.

For SAR imagery: self-supervised vision transformers for aircraft detection without manual labeling.

The relevant science: SimCLR, MAE, Whisper architectures, DINOv2.

**Multi-modal fusion**

Your data is genuinely multi-modal — text (registries, MEL items), audio (ATC), image (SAR), structured time series (ADS-B), graph (entity relationships). The cutting edge in ML is fusing these modalities into unified representations.

The relevant science: CLIP-style cross-modal contrastive learning, multimodal transformers, late fusion architectures.

For aviation specifically, this means a system that can reason jointly across "ATC says emergency divert, ADS-B shows altitude descent, registry says aircraft is operated by X, parts data says recent engine work" rather than treating each as separate signal.

**Physics-informed machine learning**

Pure data-driven ML works when you have lots of labeled data. Aviation often doesn't. But aviation also has rich physical models — flight dynamics, engine thermodynamics, structural fatigue physics. Physics-informed neural networks combine physical equations as soft constraints with data-driven learning.

The relevant science: physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), neural ODEs, hybrid physics-ML models.

For OSA: instead of pure data-driven engine wear prediction (needs lots of failure data you don't have), combine known engine physics models with observed utilization to produce physically plausible predictions even with limited training data.

**Bayesian deep learning for uncertainty quantification**

Every prediction your platform makes should come with calibrated uncertainty. "This aircraft will need a hot section overhaul in 200 hours, ±50 hours, 90% confidence." Uncertainty matters enormously for aviation decisions because the cost of being wrong differs by direction (early replacement wastes money, late replacement causes AOG).

The relevant science: variational inference for neural networks, Monte Carlo dropout, conformal prediction, deep ensembles.

For OSA: lets you communicate predictions honestly and lets buyers price decisions based on actual risk profiles.

**Active learning and optimal experimental design**

You can't observe everything. Where do you place your next SDR receiver? Which operator partnership would most improve your predictions? Which aircraft should you task SAR imagery for? Active learning says "the next observation that maximizes expected information gain about the things I care about."

The relevant science: Bayesian optimal experimental design, information-theoretic active learning, multi-armed bandit allocation.

For OSA: lets you allocate scarce resources (SDR deployment budget, SAR imagery purchases, partnership effort) based on which observations would most improve the intelligence layer's predictive accuracy.

- Anomaly detection on observation streams

**Decision layer: causal inference and active learning**

Tools for answering causal questions ("would this intervention work?") and optimizing observation allocation ("where should we invest next?"). This layer is what differentiates intelligence from analytics.

**Product layer: intelligence outputs**

The actual products customers see — predictions, alerts, dashboards, APIs. Built on top of the layers below.

## The data science gaps specifically

Beyond infrastructure, there are specific data science approaches JETNET doesn't appear to use:

**11. Bayesian state estimation for fleet dynamics**

Treating each aircraft as a system with a hidden state (component time-since-overhaul, accumulated wear, current condition) that evolves through time, observed through partial signals (ADS-B, transactions, maintenance events). Apply Bayesian filtering (Kalman, particle, or variational) to estimate state continuously.

**The OSA structural advantage**: continuously-updated estimates of every aircraft's true state, with calibrated uncertainty. This is what GE's digital twins do for engines. Applying the same math at fleet scale is a category of intelligence JETNET doesn't approach.

**12. Survival analysis for component reliability**

Statistical methods specifically designed for time-to-event data with censoring. Weibull models for component lifetimes. Cox proportional hazards for utilization-adjusted risk. Bayesian survival analysis for combining engineering priors with observed data.

**The OSA structural advantage**: principled component reliability modeling instead of "average hours between failures" estimates.

**13. Hierarchical forecasting**

Forecast demand at multiple levels (fleet, region, operator, tail) with consistency constraints. Modern algorithms (Hyndman et al., MinT reconciliation) ensure forecasts at different aggregation levels are coherent.

**The OSA structural advantage**: consistent demand forecasts across levels enables better decision-making at every level. Distributors get fleet-aggregate forecasts; operators get operator-specific forecasts; both are guaranteed to be consistent.

**14. Causal inference vs correlation**

Most analytics are correlational. Causal inference frameworks (Pearl's structural causal models, DoWhy, EconML) let you answer counterfactual questions: "would switching MROs actually reduce turnaround time, or are faster operators just more likely to choose this MRO?"

**The OSA structural advantage**: customers don't want to know what correlates with their outcomes — they want to know what causes them. Causal answers drive better decisions.

**15. Active learning and optimal data collection**

Information-theoretic approaches to deciding which observations to collect next. Where should the next SDR go? Which operator partnership would most improve predictions? Which aircraft should we task SAR imagery for?

**The OSA structural advantage**: capital allocation decisions for observation infrastructure (Beachhead Network expansion, partnership prioritization, data investment) become data-driven rather than guess-driven.

**16. Self-supervised representation learning**

Learn useful representations from unlabeled data through pretext tasks. Apply to ADS-B trajectories (contrastive learning of similar flight patterns), ATC audio (Whisper-style pre-training), aviation imagery (DINOv2-style vision transformers).

**The OSA structural advantage**: extract useful features from massive unlabeled aviation data without expensive labeling effort.

**17. Graph neural networks on the entity graph**

Once you have the unified operator-aircraft-parts-event graph, GNNs let you do inference across graph structure. Predict missing relationships. Score new entities by graph similarity. Learn embeddings that capture relational patterns.

**The OSA structural advantage**: leverages the graph structure as a feature space, dramatically improving predictions on entities you have limited direct data about.

**18. Continuous deployment and experimentation**

Modern ML practice includes A/B testing of models in production, multi-armed bandit allocation for serving, automated model retraining pipelines, experimentation platforms for measuring impact.

**The OSA structural advantage**: the intelligence layer continuously improves through systematic experimentation rather than periodic batch retraining.
