**Layer 3 — DOT Part 295 Broker Registration**

This is the federal piece. Before offering any services, you must register with the DOT Office of Aviation Analysis. Registration includes your legal and trade names, business address, and the names of your owners and key personnel.

Key compliance requirements under Part 295:

Before a client is bound by contract, you must disclose the identity of the direct air carrier operating the flight, that you're a broker and not the carrier, and any business relationship between you and the carrier that could bear on carrier selection.

All advertising must clearly state you're a broker, not an air carrier in operational control of the aircraft.

Under Part 295, you can operate in one of three capacities: as an indirect air carrier (principal in the transaction), as agent of the charterer, or as agent of the air carrier. As an indirect air carrier, you can contract in your own right with an air carrier and resell the charter as a principal to your customer. Your "agent-of-the-customer" model is one of these defined capacities.

**Important note:** Part 295 doesn't actually require licensing or establish a formal registry — it sets disclosure and conduct rules. But registration with DOT's Office of Aviation Analysis is still required. This is exactly why having your aviation attorney draft your disclosure templates, contract language, and website compliance copy is worth the money upfront.

He's asking about trademark protection, not patents. Patents are for inventions. Trademarks protect names and logos. I should clarify this gently and give him the practical roadmap.Quick clarification — you want a **trademark**, not a patent. Patents protect inventions. Trademarks protect names, logos, and brand identifiers. Easy mix-up.

Here's the move:

**USPTO Trademark Registration**

You'd file with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) at uspto.gov. You can file two separate trademark applications — one for the wordmark "Orange Sunshine" (protects the name itself regardless of font/style), and one for the logo (protects the specific visual design).

Filing options: you can file under "Intent to Use" (Section 1b) since you're pre-revenue — meaning you plan to use it in commerce but haven't yet. Once you launch and start transacting, you submit a "Statement of Use" and the mark goes live. Alternatively, if you're already using it on your website or marketing materials, you can file under "Use in Commerce" (Section 1a) now.

Filing fees run about $250-350 per class per mark through TEAS (the USPTO online system). You'd file under **Class 39** (transportation services / arranging air transportation) at minimum. Possibly Class 36 too (financial services) if you want to protect the payment infrastructure side early.

**Before you file**, do a trademark search on USPTO's TESS database to make sure "Orange Sunshine" isn't already registered in your classes. Given the name's history (the LSD association), it's possible someone has it in other classes, but aviation/transportation is likely clear.

**Practical sequence:**

1. Search TESS at uspto.gov
2. File Intent-to-Use for "Orange Sunshine" wordmark (Class 39)
3. File separately for the logo mark
4. Convert to Use in Commerce once you close your first deal
5. Consider Class 36 filing when the payment infrastructure goes live

The whole process takes 8-12 months for approval. You can use the ™ symbol immediately upon filing. The ® symbol comes only after registration is granted.

Worth noting — this is another place your attorney earns their fee. A trademark attorney can do the search more thoroughly and handle office actions if the USPTO pushes back. But the initial TESS search you can do yourself in five minutes to gut-check it.
