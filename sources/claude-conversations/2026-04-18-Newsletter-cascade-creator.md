## The Loud Hawk Waterfall

**Core principle: the newsletter is the top of the waterfall, not the bottom.**

Dan Koe's whole thing is that the long-form piece is where the real thinking happens. Everything downstream is reformatting, not rewriting. Your editorial blueprint is already built for this — every edition has a Main Piece (600-900 words) that's sharp and specific. That's your source of truth. X posts and carousels are extractions from it, not parallel effort.

## The Flow

**Sunday — Newsletter publishes** (biweekly, per your calendar)

The Main Piece is the pillar. Everything for the next two weeks comes out of it. Before hitting send, you mine the edition for five things:
- **One sharp thesis line** — the single sentence that makes the whole piece worth reading
- **Three supporting claims** — specific observations, numbers, or patterns that back the thesis
- **One contrarian angle** — the thing "the market gets wrong" that appears in The Gap lane
- **One mechanics moment** — the How It Actually Works detail
- **One quote-worthy line** — the sentence someone would screenshot

That's your raw material. Five extractable units per edition.

**Monday — X Thread + carousel (Publish Week, Drop 1)**

The thread is the thesis + three claims, one post per beat. Five to seven posts. Open with the thesis line as a standalone hook. Close with "full edition here → loudhawk.media/[slug]". This is the widest-reach version.

The carousel is the same material, visual-first. Your Loud Hawk design system (Briquete for manuscript, Lexend for text, Deep Purple + orange) already handles this. 6-8 slides. Slide 1 is the thesis. Slides 2-5 are the claims with supporting visuals/numbers. Slide 6 is the unexpected angle. Slide 7 is the CTA to subscribe.

**Wednesday — X single post + LinkedIn (Publish Week, Drop 2)**

Single X post = the quote-worthy line as a standalone. No thread, no link. Pure credibility signal. This is what gets screenshotted.

LinkedIn version of the same post, expanded to 150-200 words with context. Per your blueprint, LinkedIn is P0 for deals — this is the conversion-adjacent channel.

**Friday — X post + carousel (Off Week, Drop 3)**

The contrarian angle as its own piece. Format: "Most people think X. Here's what actually happens." This is The Gap lane doing its job.

Carousel version if the angle has visual data backing it (route pricing, fleet comp, etc.). Text-only post if it's a pure take.

**Monday (off week) — X single post (Off Week, Drop 4)**

The mechanics moment. Format: "Here's how [specific industry process] actually works." Pure How It Actually Works lane. Can be a thread if it needs 3-4 beats, but usually a single post works.

**Wednesday (off week) — Carousel (Off Week, Drop 5)**

The fifth extractable — usually a data visualization or a "Cost Anatomy" style breakdown. This is the shareable, save-worthy version. Highest Instagram performance historically.

## What this gets you per edition

- 1 newsletter (pillar)
- 1 X thread (5-7 posts)
- 3-4 X single posts
- 2-3 carousels
- 1-2 LinkedIn posts (expanded versions of X content)

Roughly 10 outputs per edition. Over a biweekly cycle, you're posting something 4-5 times a week without writing 4-5 times a week.

Every X post and every carousel should be traceable back to a specific line or claim in the edition. If you can't point to it, it doesn't ship.

## The case for weekly

## The case against (the honest version)

**The original blueprint deliberately chose biweekly for a reason.** Per the 60-day calendar: publish week has one job (ship), off week has one job (observe). The premise was that founder content dies when the founder can't sustain it. Biweekly was the cadence that wouldn't break you while you're flying E175 lines and building Dumont.

**Your waterfall works because the edition is dense.** A 600-900 word Main Piece has five extractable units. A 400-word weekly that got written in a rush has maybe two. Thin editions make for thin waterfalls.

## The actual recommendation

**Don't go weekly from biweekly in one jump. Go weekly with a structural change.**

Split the weekly into two formats:

**Week A — Full edition** (your current biweekly format, 600-900 word Main Piece, full structure). This is the pillar. Full waterfall extracts from it.

**Week B — Signal** (100-200 words, one observation, one chart or data point, no full structure). This was already in the 60-day calendar as a post-review option: "Loud Hawk Signal — 100–150 words, one market observation, no full structure."

Week B is the cadence insurance. It's short enough that you can ship it even when you're flying a 4-day trip. It gives readers a weekly drumbeat. And it still produces 2-3 extractable units for the waterfall (quote-worthy line + one X single post + one carousel if the data is visual).

**What the waterfall looks like at weekly cadence:**

- **Week A (Full edition):** 1 newsletter, 1 X thread, 2-3 X single posts, 2 carousels, 1 LinkedIn = ~7 outputs
- **Week B (Signal):** 1 newsletter, 1 X single post, 1 carousel, 1 LinkedIn = ~4 outputs

Over 4 weeks: 4 newsletters + ~22 social outputs. Versus biweekly: 2 newsletters + ~20 social outputs. You double newsletter output with only ~10% more social work, because the waterfall is doing the heavy lifting.

## The honest gut check

The question isn't really "biweekly vs weekly." It's "can you ship a Main Piece that clears the blueprint scorecard every week?" If the answer is no, weekly breaks the brand. If the answer is yes, biweekly is leaving compounding on the table.

Given that you're building HAWK (Scout → Writer → Designer → Conductor) specifically to reduce the per-edition cost, the right sequencing might be: stay biweekly until HAWK v1 is live, then flip to weekly with the A/B format split the moment the agent system is pulling its weight. Don't increase cadence before you've lowered the marginal cost of an edition.

What's driving the question — is it about growing the list faster, or is it about the waterfall feeling under-fed at biweekly?

## Forget cadence. Start with constraints.

There are four real constraints that any Loud Hawk content system has to survive:

**1. You fly an E175 for a living.** You have 4-day trips where you can barely open your laptop. You have 3-day breaks where you can work 10-12 hours. The system has to absorb this variance, not fight it. Fixed cadences (weekly, biweekly) assume uniform weeks. You don't have uniform weeks.

**2. Insight is the scarce resource, not output.** You can generate infinite posts. You cannot generate infinite *sharp* observations about private aviation that a competing broker couldn't write. The bottleneck is never "what should I post today." It's "what do I actually know that's worth saying."

**3. You already have proprietary inputs nobody else has.** Dumont Intelligence data. Every operator call. Every quote you price. Every route you analyze. Every conversation with a post-raise founder. These are signal streams that never stop generating raw material — you just aren't capturing them systematically.

**4. The waterfall model is backwards for you.** Dan Koe's model works because he sits at a desk and *writes*. His insight and his output happen in the same motion. You generate insight while flying, while on operator calls, while running Dumont queries — and then lose most of it because the capture layer doesn't exist.

## The first-principles reframe

Loud Hawk's content system shouldn't be a *publishing schedule*. It should be a **signal capture and refinement pipeline** where publishing is just the final stage.

The question isn't "when do we publish?" It's "how do we make sure every sharp observation you have makes it from your brain to the reader without being lost?"

That reframes the entire system.

## The pipeline (4 stages)

**Stage 1 — Capture (continuous, zero-friction)**

Every signal gets captured the moment it happens. Not "written up" — *captured*. Voice memo, one-line note, Dumont query screenshot, chat paste. The rule: if it takes more than 30 seconds, the capture layer is broken.

Sources:
- Voice memos during walks, layovers, drives
- Operator calls — one-line takeaway per call, dropped into a single inbox
- Dumont queries — any anomaly or pattern you notice gets tagged
- Client conversations — one-line learning per conversation
- Industry news — a reaction note, not a share (reactions are proprietary, shares are commodity)
- Your actual work building OSA — every decision you make is itself a signal

Target: 20-40 raw captures per week. No filtering at capture stage. Garbage in is fine — refinement happens later.

**Stage 2 — Refine (scheduled, short sessions)**

Once a week, 30-60 minutes, you triage the inbox. Not to write — to *sort*. Every capture gets one of four tags mapped to your existing lanes:

- **MS** — Market Signal (pricing, demand, fleet, routes)
- **HIAW** — How It Actually Works (mechanics, process, industry reality)
- **TG** — The Gap (what the market gets wrong)
- **SA** — Situational Awareness (timely, this-week)

And one of three quality grades:

- **A** — This is worth a Main Piece. Has legs. Proprietary. Specific.
- **B** — This is worth a short post or a carousel. Real but not enough depth for a full edition.
- **C** — Archive. Might combine with others later, might die.

Output of Stage 2: a living backlog where any given moment you can see "here are my A-grade pieces waiting to be written, here are my B-grade pieces waiting to ship, here's what's been observed but not yet processed."

This is the thing biweekly/weekly debates miss entirely. **The backlog is the publication.** Cadence is just a function of how fast the backlog grows.

**Stage 3 — Compose (on-demand, when A-grade piece is ready)**

An A-grade capture becomes a Main Piece *when it's ready*, not on a calendar. Sometimes that's 3 days from capture, sometimes 3 weeks. The quality scorecard from the blueprint is the gate, not the date.

When a Main Piece ships, the waterfall from our earlier conversation kicks in — 5 extractable units, distributed across X/LinkedIn/carousel over the following 7-10 days.

B-grade captures ship as standalone X posts, carousels, or LinkedIn posts *between* main pieces. These don't need the full refinement — a B-grade capture is usually 80% ready as-is. One pass of polish, it's out.

**Stage 4 — Distribute (automated, consistent drumbeat)**

Every day has social output, but only because the backlog is feeding it. No empty days, because B-grade captures fill the gaps between Main Pieces. No forced Main Pieces, because an A-grade capture can wait two weeks if it isn't ready.

Social distribution is *constant*. Newsletter cadence is *variable but tracked*. The reader-facing illusion is of a highly consistent publication. The builder-facing reality is a pipeline that flexes with your actual life.

## Why this wins

**It matches your life.** On a 4-day trip you capture (Stage 1, zero friction). On a 3-day break you refine, compose, and queue (Stages 2-3). The system doesn't break when you fly.

**It honors the insight bottleneck.** You never ship a Main Piece because the calendar said so. You ship when something is actually ready. The quality bar from the blueprint stays intact.

**It turns your proprietary inputs into product.** Dumont, operator calls, client conversations — these already exist. The system just makes sure they flow into the publication instead of dying in your head.

**It kills the cadence anxiety.** Weekly vs biweekly vs whatever — irrelevant. If the pipeline is healthy, you're shipping a Main Piece every 7-14 days naturally. If the pipeline is starved, forcing a weekly edition just ships weaker content. The pipeline tells you the truth.

**It maps directly to HAWK.** Scout is Stage 1 (capture from Dumont, news, signals). Writer is Stage 3 (compose Main Pieces from A-grade captures). Designer + Conductor is Stage 4 (waterfall distribution). Analyst is the feedback loop that tells you which lanes and which captures are converting. The agent system you already designed *is* this pipeline. You just hadn't named the stages yet.

## The one decision you still have to make

Not cadence. Not structure. This:

**What's your capture mechanism?**

Everything in this system depends on Stage 1 being *actually zero-friction*. If you have to open an app, file something correctly, or "process" it at capture time — it fails. The moment the capture layer has friction, the pipeline starves and you're back to staring at a blank page every Sunday.

Voice memo → automatic transcript → single inbox is one option. A dedicated Telegram channel (you already use Telegram for Hermes) is another. A Drafts app hotkey is a third. The mechanism matters less than the principle: one input, zero decisions, always available, including in the cockpit.

## Claude

Quick definitions:

**Single post** — one standalone tweet, 280 characters max (or up to 25,000 if you have X Premium, but the 280-char version is what most people read and share). It's a complete thought that stands on its own. No follow-up required. Reader sees it, gets the point, moves on.

**Thread** — a series of connected posts, chained together, meant to be read in sequence. The first post hooks the reader, each subsequent post builds the argument, and the last post usually delivers the payoff or the CTA. Threads can be 3 posts or 30. Typical Loud Hawk thread would be 5-8 posts.

## How they differ for Loud Hawk specifically

**Single post** is for:
- A sharp, quotable observation that lands in one sentence
- "Here's a fact that will surprise you" moments
- Engagement-driven content (replies, quote-tweets)
- The screenshot-worthy line from a Main Edition
- Reactive industry commentary

Example: *"Light jet empty legs on Northeast corridors dropped 34% YoY in Q1. Nobody is talking about why."*

That stands alone. It's a hook, a data point, and an implicit invitation to reply — all in one post. If someone wants more, they click your profile or your pinned link.

**Thread** is for:
- An argument with multiple supporting claims
- A walkthrough of how something works
- A build-in-public story with a beginning, middle, end
- Extracted content from a Main Edition or Deep Dive
- Anything that needs more than one beat to land

Example thread structure from a Loud Hawk Main Edition:

> Post 1 (hook): "Light jet empty legs on Northeast corridors dropped 34% YoY in Q1 2026. Here's what's actually happening →"
>
> Post 2: "The narrative says demand is down. The data says something different..."
>
> Post 3: "[specific data point + why it matters]"
>
> Post 4: "[second claim with evidence]"
>
> Post 5: "[contrarian angle - the thing the market is missing]"
>
> Post 6: "[implication - what this means for charter clients]"
>
> Post 7 (close): "Full breakdown in this week's Loud Hawk → [link]"

Seven posts, one coherent argument, clear payoff at the end with a link back to the newsletter.

## Why both matter in the waterfall

They do different jobs:

**Threads drive newsletter subscribers.** The thread is where someone new to Loud Hawk sees the full quality of your thinking and gets convinced to subscribe. The last post is always a "full edition here →" link. Threads are the conversion vehicle.

**Single posts build habit and reach.** Not everyone will read a thread — but everyone scrolling will stop on a single sharp post if the hook lands. Single posts get quote-tweeted, screenshotted, replied-to. They're how Loud Hawk becomes a name people recognize before they subscribe. Single posts are the reach vehicle.

## The practical waterfall split

From every Main Edition:
- **1 thread** (published Monday, the same day the newsletter ships — announces and summarizes the edition, drives subscribers)
- **3 single posts** (spread Tuesday/Wednesday/Thursday — each extracts one quotable line or claim from the edition, no link, pure insight)

Threads do the conversion work. Single posts do the presence work. You need both.

## One more distinction worth knowing

**Quote-tweet** is a third format — when you share someone else's post with your own commentary on top. Not really part of the waterfall from your own content, but important for Loud Hawk because it's how you engage with industry news, competitor moves, or trending conversations. Quote-tweet an aviation news story with a Loud Hawk take → positions the publication as a voice in the conversation. Usually once or twice a week when something relevant happens.

**The pattern:** as soon as the newsletter becomes a *brand*, the publisher wants the site to be the brand — not a Substack subdomain or a Beehiiv-hosted page.

**Pattern 2 — Substack only (starter, creator-focused)**

Substack as both the publishing platform AND the website. The publication lives at something.substack.com (or a custom domain pointed at Substack). Simpler. Faster. Built-in discovery through Substack's network. Native payments for paid tiers.

- Solo writers starting out

- Publications that prioritize writing > design

Tradeoffs:
- Less design control — everything looks like Substack
- Discovery is Substack's algorithm (good for growth, bad for independence)
- Substack takes 10% of paid subscription revenue
- You don't fully own the reader experience

**Pattern 3 — Custom website + Substack as one of several channels**

Rare but real. Some publications run a custom site AND have a Substack presence for distribution/discovery (like they'd have a YouTube channel). This is more common for writers who want to tap into Substack's network for growth while maintaining their own home base.
