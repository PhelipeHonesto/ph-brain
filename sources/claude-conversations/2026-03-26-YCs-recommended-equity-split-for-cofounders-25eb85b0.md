# YC's recommended equity split for cofounders

*Summary: **Conversation Overview**

Phelipe, the founder of OSA (a charter brokerage and aircraft transaction company) and Loud Hawk (a content/intelligence platform), engaged in a detailed conversation about startup equity mechanics and the competitive landscape in private aviation. The conversation began with foundational questions about YC's equity split recommendations (equal splits among cofounders) and option pool structuring, covering how pools should be sized at each funding stage (10% at incorporation, expanding to 15–20% by Series A and beyond) and what revenue and growth milestones typically trigger each round from pre-seed through Series C+.

The conversation then shifted to competitive intelligence. Phelipe asked about Hamilton AI, a seed-stage competitor that raised $9.8M total (pre-seed plus seed) to build an AI-powered operator SaaS platform for private aviation. Claude analyzed their funding structure, noting that the aggressive valuation is largely justified by the serial founder's prior track record rather than current traction, but flagged the risk of growing into an inflated valuation. flyExclusive's acquisition of Volato's Mission Control IP and the launch of Contrails — a free flight management system for operators — was identified as a significant threat to Hamilton AI's paid SaaS model, while potentially benefiting OSA as a broker by improving operator network transparency.

Phelipe then prompted a broader scan of funded aviation companies, leading to a comprehensive mapping of the competitive landscape including Bond ($400M raised, Bombardier fleet order), Flexjet ($800M from L Catterton), Wheels Up (fleet modernization but financial distress), FlyHouse (seeking $300M at aggressive projections), TLC Jet (consolidation play), and Capstone Jet Charter (advisory-first brokerage model similar to OSA's Safety Brief concept). The conversation culminated in Phelipe's sharp observation that no funded startup is building a true full-stack brokerage combining charter and aircraft transactions under one integrated service model — a gap that defines OSA's strategic positioning. Claude confirmed this analysis, mapping how every funded player has chosen a single vertical (operator, tech platform, marketplace, charter-only, or transactions-only), and framed OSA's integrated architecture — OSA Connect, OSA Aircraft, and Loud Hawk feeding both funnels — as genuine whitespace that is better suited to a founder-operator build than a VC-scale play.*

## Claude

The user is asking about how much equity pool should be reserved (likely for employees/options). Let me answer from what I know about YC's recommendations and standard startup practice.Standard YC/startup guidance on option pools:

**10–20% is the typical range**, with 10% being most common at the seed stage. Here's how it usually breaks down:

**At incorporation/pre-seed:** Reserve 10% in an option pool. This covers your first ~10 hires and early advisors.

**At seed round:** Investors often ask you to expand the pool to 15–20% *before* their investment (so dilution comes from founders, not investors). This is a negotiation point.

**How to allocate within the pool:**
- Early engineering/key hires: 0.5–2% each
- Advisors: 0.25–1% each (with a 2-year vesting schedule)
- Later employees: progressively smaller grants as the company grows and shares become more valuable

**Vesting matters as much as the split.** Standard is 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff — meaning nothing vests until month 12, then monthly after that. This protects you if someone leaves early.

For OSA specifically, since you're solo and pre-revenue, I'd keep it simple: set aside 10% on paper now, don't actually grant any of it until you have a specific hire or advisor worth issuing to. No reason to complicate the cap table before you need to.

## Claude

The user wants a breakdown of how the option pool typically looks at each funding stage.Here's the typical progression:

**Pre-Seed / Incorporation**
- Founder(s): 90%
- Option pool: 10%
- Most of the pool is unallocated, maybe 1–2% granted to a key early hire or advisor

**Seed Round**
- Founders: ~70–75%
- Investors: ~15–20%
- Option pool: 10–15% (investors often require a top-up before they invest)
- A few grants issued, most still reserved for future hires

**Series A**
- Founders: ~50–55%
- All investors combined: ~30–35%
- Option pool: 15–20% (refreshed again pre-round)
- Pool is actively being used — early hires vesting, new grants for senior leadership

**Series B+**
- Founders: ~30–40%
- All investors: ~40–50%
- Option pool: 10–15% (refreshed as needed)
- Individual grants get smaller as share price increases — a 0.1% grant at Series B can be worth more than a 1% grant at seed

**Key pattern to notice:** Investors almost always ask you to refresh the pool *before* their money comes in. That dilution falls on existing shareholders (you). This is one of the biggest negotiation levers at each round — push to keep the pool as small as you realistically need for the next 12–18 months of hiring, not what the investor wants as a cushion.

For OSA's stage, 10% reserved and mostly unallocated is the right move until you're actively raising or hiring equity-compensated people.

**Series A**
- Revenue: $1M–$3M ARR
- Growth: 2–3x year-over-year
- What matters: repeatable sales motion, clear unit economics, low churn
- This is the "prove it scales" round

**Series B**
- Revenue: $5M–$15M ARR
- Growth: 2x+ YoY sustained
- What matters: efficient growth, strong margins, a team that executes without founder doing everything

**Series C+**
- Revenue: $20M–$50M+ ARR
- What matters: market leadership trajectory, path to profitability, expansion opportunities

**The honest truth though:** these are guidelines, not rules. A compelling founder with deep domain expertise in a big market (aviation is a $300B+ industry) can raise earlier than the metrics suggest. Conversely, hitting the numbers with weak fundamentals won't get you funded.

For your situation, the more practical question might be: at what point does OSA Connect generate enough commission revenue that you need capital to capture demand you're already turning away? That's when raising makes strategic sense rather than dilutive.
