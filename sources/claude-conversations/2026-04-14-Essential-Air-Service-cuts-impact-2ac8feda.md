## Claude

Phelipe is asking how EAS cuts could affect SkyWest. Let me think through this carefully given what I know about SkyWest's business model.

SkyWest operates under two distinct models:
1. **Capacity Purchase Agreements (CPAs)** with United, Delta, American, Alaska — this is their mainline regional flying (E175, CRJ series). This is NOT EAS.
2. **EAS flying** — SkyWest does participate in EAS contracts, serving ~40 communities as shown in the chart.

Let me think through the implications.SkyWest has two distinct exposure points here:

**Direct EAS exposure**
- SkyWest serves ~40 EAS communities — more than any other carrier on that chart
- At $90.7/seat, they're the most efficient, but a 33% cut to subsidy rates could make some routes financially unviable even for them
- Contract terminations would mean immediate route shutdowns, aircraft redeployment headaches, and crew displacement

**Indirect / strategic exposure**
- SkyWest's core business is CPAs (United, Delta, American, Alaska) — that's the E175 flying you do, and it's insulated from EAS cuts
- But EAS flying fills utilization gaps and keeps crews current on thinner routes — losing it tightens their operational flexibility
- If small communities lose air service entirely, it accelerates the narrative that regional aviation is structurally broken, which puts pressure on CPA negotiations and pilot recruitment/retention optics

**The pilot angle (relevant to you personally)**
- EAS route losses historically trigger furloughs or reduced hours at regional carriers
- SkyWest is better capitalized than most, but the domino effect — smaller EAS carriers collapsing, pilots flooding the market — could compress upgrade timelines industry-wide

**Bottom line:** SkyWest won't collapse over this, but they'd likely exit the least profitable EAS routes quietly and redeploy those aircraft to CPA flying where the economics are guaranteed. The communities they drop will simply lose service.
