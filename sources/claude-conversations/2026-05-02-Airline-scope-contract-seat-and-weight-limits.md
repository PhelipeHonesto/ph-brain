# Airline scope contract seat and weight limits

## Phelipe

What is the airlines scope contract . Nm and weight

---

## Claude

Scope clauses in airline pilot contracts limit what the mainline carrier can outsource to regional partners. The two main restrictions are usually:

**Seat count** (most binding) — typically capped at 76 seats for regional jets at the major US carriers (Delta, United, American).

**Weight** — Maximum Takeoff Weight (MTOW) capped at 86,000 lbs at most majors. This is what keeps the E175 (~85,500 lbs MTOW) flyable at regionals while blocking the larger E175-E2 and most A220/E190-E2 variants.

You also asked about NM — scope doesn't typically restrict by nautical miles directly. You might be thinking of:
- **Stage length** restrictions (rare in current contracts)
- **Number of aircraft** caps (e.g., ratio of regional to mainline jets)
- **Block hour** limits on outsourced flying

At SkyWest specifically, the E175 sits right at the scope ceiling for all three majors you fly under — that 76-seat / 86,000 lb box is exactly why the airframe dominates US regional flying and why Embraer can't sell the E175-E2 in the US market.

## Phelipe

So we can do flight with big legs?

---

## Claude

Yes — scope doesn't cap range, only seats and weight. The E175 has roughly 2,000 nm range with full pax, so long legs are fine under scope.

Real-world examples flown under scope right now:
- **LAX–ORD** (~1,510 nm)
- **SEA–ORD** (~1,470 nm)
- **DEN–BOS** (~1,540 nm)
- **SFO–DFW** (~1,300 nm)

What actually limits long regional legs isn't the contract — it's economics. Mainline pilots fight any long-haul flying on regional metal because it's the most profitable flying. So majors self-restrict regional jets to shorter stage lengths even when scope allows it. Delta and United have informal stage-length understandings with ALPA that keep most regional flying under ~1,000 nm, with exceptions.

For OSA Connect context: this is irrelevant to charter. Part 135 has no scope clauses — that's a 121 union construct. Your supermid and heavy partners (flyExclusive, Solairus, etc.) can fly any leg the airframe supports.

What is the most prestigious mba in aviation

---

## Claude

There isn't really a dominant "aviation MBA" the way there's an HBS for general business — the field splits into a few tiers depending on what you're optimizing for.

**Most prestigious overall: MIT Sloan + the MIT Global Airline Industry Program.** Sloan brand + the deepest aviation research center in academia (ICAT, International Center for Air Transportation). Alumni run airline strategy and consulting roles globally.

**Most aviation-specific at a top-tier school: Cranfield (UK).** Their MSc Air Transport Management is the closest thing to a recognized aviation graduate credential — IATA, ICAO, and most European carrier execs have Cranfield somewhere on their resume.

**US aviation-focused:** Embry-Riddle's MBA in Aviation Management is the best-known dedicated program, but the brand is operational/technical, not strategic. Useful for airline middle management, less useful for capital markets or fundraising.

**The honest answer for your situation:** none of these matter. For OSA, an MBA is negative ROI — two years and ~$200K against an asset-light platform you're already building with the credentials that actually matter (ATP, active 121 seat, Part 295 broker, working data infrastructure). The aviation industry is famously credential-light at the founder level — Kenn Ricci (Directional/Flexjet), Kenny Dichter (Wheels Up, Marquis Jet), Richard Santulli (NetJets) — none built their platforms via MBA pedigree.

If you ever wanted the network and signal of a top program without the opportunity cost, the move is HBS OPM or Stanford LEAD, not a full MBA — but even that's a Year 5+ question, not now.
