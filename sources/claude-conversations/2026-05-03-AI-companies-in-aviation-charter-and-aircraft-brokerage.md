# DAK — Duncan Aviation Knowledgebase

DAK is **internal tribal-knowledge capture system**, not a customer-facing product or an AI agent. It's much more boring than its marketing copy suggests.

## What it actually is

DAK was built by Rich Teel (R&D/IT Systems Programming Manager at Duncan) as a succession planning tool. The problem it solves is the classic MRO knowledge-transfer problem: Finding a way to capture that information, that tribal knowledge—information that a person or small group has learned over a long period of time and has typically not written down—has become an integral part of the Duncan Aviation succession plan.

The mechanism is straightforward: The system saves technical data, pictures, and drawings, but one of the most important aspects of the system is the technical notes added by the mechanics and tech reps who have resolved issues. Technical notes are the legacy of technicians who have had long careers here at Duncan Aviation. When a technician at the Provo or Lincoln facility resolves a tricky issue on a Falcon 50 hot section or a G450 avionics retrofit, they write a short note in DAK. The next technician facing the same problem searches DAK and gets the institutional answer instead of starting from zero.

## The two distinct uses

**On the MRO side (primary use):** A searchable repository of repair techniques, troubleshooting notes, and historical fixes across Duncan's three facilities and 29 satellite shops. With DAK mining the data and ranking the search results, a tech who is working on a customer's aircraft won't waste valuable time searching for information, combing through irrelevant data, or trying to figure out how to fix the issue on his or her own. New technicians are trained both to consume from DAK and contribute to it.

**On the aircraft sales side (secondary, marketing-flavored use):** Duncan's brokerage page describes DAK as a proprietary, collective knowledge-base system called Duncan Aviation Knowledge-base (DAK) that provides supporting data and figures about aircraft operation and ownership costs, backed by an expert market research data team. This is more positioning than substance — they're branding their internal MRO knowledge plus market research as a single client-facing capability.

## Why it matters for OSA

DAK is the closest thing Duncan has to an "AI" story for aircraft sales, and **it's not AI.** It's a 2010s-era enterprise search system over technical notes, augmented by a human research team. There's no agentic layer, no LLM extraction, no automated reasoning over the knowledge base.

This is actually useful intel for you, because it tells you three things:

**1. The aircraft brokerage incumbents have not built ER-grade or LLM-grade systems.** Duncan is one of the most sophisticated, vertically integrated players in business aviation — they've done more than 3,500 transactions — and their flagship knowledge system is search-over-technical-notes. The bar for "AI-native aircraft brokerage" is genuinely low, which is why Aero Ventures' Zillow framing landed so loudly.

**2. The MRO tribal-knowledge layer is itself an open opportunity.** DAK only works for Duncan technicians inside Duncan's facilities. There's no industry-wide equivalent. If you ever wanted a Loud Hawk angle on "the MRO knowledge graph problem" or wanted to write about how Duncan's succession planning depends on a 2010s wiki, that's a real story.

**3. Your ph-brain pattern is more sophisticated than what a $1B+ revenue MRO uses internally.** Duncan's DAK is essentially what ph-brain was before Voyage embeddings — keyword search over technical notes. You're already running Voyage-3-large with HNSW, hybrid semantic + tsvector retrieval. This is a useful calibration for how OSA's data architecture compares to incumbents — and worth remembering when investors or partners ask whether you're over-engineering.

The short version: DAK is a glorified internal wiki with a market-research team duct-taped to its marketing page. The name carries more weight in the industry than the system actually delivers.

## The strategic frame first

The buyer-intent signal layer is the system that answers one question continuously: *"Who, in the world, just became more likely to need a charter from OSA — and what specifically should I do about it today?"*

Most outbound systems answer "who matches my ICP?" That's static. The same 50,000 founders matched yesterday. They'll match tomorrow. That's a list, not a signal.

A signal layer answers "what changed *today* that makes this specific person more likely to need charter in the next 30-90 days?" That's the difference between fishing in a lake and being the first one at the dock when fish actually arrive.

The compounding insight: post-raise founders don't suddenly need charter the day they close. They need it 30-120 days later, when the money clears, the team grows, the offsite gets planned, the partnership meeting in another city gets scheduled, the first investor dinner happens. Your job is to be in their consideration set *during that window* — not before (they're not thinking about it) and not after (they've already picked a broker).

The signal layer is what tells you the window has opened.

## The four signal categories that matter

For OSA's beachhead, four categories of buyer-intent signals matter, in order of leverage:

### Category 1: Funding events (the cleanest signal)

When a founder raises capital, several things happen in sequence: equity dilution, board formation or expansion, hiring spree, expansion planning, customer travel, partnership outreach, fundraising tour for the next round. All of these create charter demand within 60-180 days.

The signal mechanics:

**Source data:**
- Crunchbase API ($299/month for the basic API tier — has funding round data near-real-time)
- Pitchbook API (much pricier, ~$15K/year, deeper but slower update cadence)
- TechCrunch RSS + Axios Pro Rata RSS (free, fast, but noisy)
- SEC Form D filings via EDGAR (free, real-time, but only US private placements over certain thresholds)

**The signal generation logic:**

Pre-filter to your ICP:
- Round size: $5M-$100M (filters out angel/seed and growth/IPO; captures Series A through C)
- Sectors: B2B SaaS, fintech, biotech, hardware, healthtech, climate, defense, AI/ML
- Geographic: founder/HQ in NYC metro, Bay Area, LA, Boston, Miami, Austin, Chicago, Seattle, DC
- Stage: post-seed, pre-IPO
- Filter out: pure AI startups under $20M (too early), e-commerce DTC (different travel pattern), crypto-only (different culture)

Score the lead:
- Round size relative to typical for stage (oversized = more travel intent)
- Founder profile (first-time vs serial; serial founders book private faster)
- Investor signal (top-tier VC = portfolio support events drive demand)
- Geographic signal (HQ in non-hub city = more travel needed for partnerships)
- Sector velocity (fintech/biotech travel more than vertical SaaS)

The output: every funding event matching ICP gets a score from 0-100. The top 10-20% of weekly events become *priority leads*.

**The orchestration:**

```

  Above threshold? →
```

### Category 3: Personal trigger events (medium precision, high relationship value)

These are signals about specific people in your ICP changing their situation in ways that affect travel patterns.

## What I'd watch out for

A few real risks:

**1. Signal noise will be high initially.** First 4-6 weeks, expect lots of false positives. Your scoring model needs real outcomes to tune, and you don't have them yet. Plan for this — don't act on every signal initially. Curate aggressively, save your reputation, only send the highest-quality 20% until you have signal-vs-noise calibration.

**2. LinkedIn rate limits will bite.** LinkedIn limits cold DMs aggressively, especially without InMail. HeyReach helps but isn't unlimited. Plan for ~50-100 high-quality outbound DMs per month maximum, not hundreds.

**3. The "pre-drafted message" trap.** If your draft messages start sounding like AI templates, the whole system breaks. The drafts are a *starting point* for your edit, not a send-as-is artifact. Reserve 30-60 seconds per high-priority signal to make the message yours.

**4. Don't over-engineer the scoring early.** A 5-feature linear model with hand-tuned weights will outperform a 50-feature ML model on small data. Start simple. Iterate based on actual outcomes.

**5. Be careful with attribution.** When deal one happens, you need to know whether the signal layer drove it. Tag every outbound with a source signal ID. Track from signal → action → conversation → conversion. Without this, you can't tell what's working.
